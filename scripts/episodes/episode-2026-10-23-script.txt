[HEADLINE ROUNDUP]

Item one. California's CCPA deletion right just got materially wider, and it applies to third-party-sourced data starting now.

Governor Newsom signed Senate Bill 923 on September 27th, just before the legislative deadline. 

SB 923, also known as the Expanding Privacy Rights Act, amends the CCPA to expand existing deletion rights by requiring businesses to delete personal information collected "from or about" a consumer, rather than only information collected directly from that consumer.

 The word change is small. The compliance surface area it creates is not. If Firecrown's brands hold behavioral data about a California reader that originated from a data broker, a co-registration partner, or an audience extension vendor, a deletion request now reaches that data. 

Data brokers themselves also face a shorter response cycle: AB 883 cuts the DELETE platform response window from forty-five to thirty days.

 Combined with the automated decision-making rules that took effect January first this year and the continuing CPPA enforcement pattern against opt-out friction, California is operating as if the cure period never existed — because, as a reminder, it doesn't. 

Unlike Virginia, Colorado, or Connecticut, the CCPA has no cure period and is enforced by a dedicated agency with audit authority in addition to the Attorney General.

 The practical action here: your data inventory map, wherever it sits, now needs a column that distinguishes first-party collected data from inferred or sourced data. Those are no longer the same liability.

Item two. The FTC puts behaviorally conditioned guardrails on Publicis's acquisition of LiveRamp. This is the deep dive topic, so headlines only here.



The proposed consent order addresses the FTC's concerns that Publicis's acquisition of LiveRamp — which provides data connectivity services for digital advertising — would harm competition in markets for media agency services and audience data services. The deal is valued at two point two billion dollars.

 

Under the order, LiveRamp must continue serving the clients of rival agencies on neutral terms, Publicis is walled off from LiveRamp customer data, and an outside auditor checks compliance every six months. The Commission voted two to zero, and a thirty-day public comment period now runs before the order can be made final.

 We will unpack exactly what this means for a publisher like Firecrown in the deep dive.

Item three. A federal magistrate judge orders Meta to name every executive who authorized downloading pirated books for AI training.



US Magistrate Judge Thomas Hixson on October 19th ordered Meta to identify every person who decided on or approved obtaining book data from pirate libraries and the use of BitTorrent, ruling that even where decisions were made by a team, the names of all those involved must be provided. Meta had argued that its early AI training data buildout was informal and that no single authorized person made the decisions, but the court rejected this.

 

Plaintiffs allege Meta obtained roughly eighty-point-six terabytes from LibGen and about thirty-five-point-seven terabytes from Anna's Archive.

 

The order, together with internal documents cited by plaintiffs indicating prior escalation to CEO Zuckerberg, intensifies scrutiny of management's role in sourcing AI training data.

 This matters for any company using third-party datasets to fine-tune models. The question is no longer whether the training was fair use. The question is now who, specifically, approved the data acquisition, and whether that decision chain has been documented. That is a governance and recordkeeping issue, not just an IP issue.

Separately on the IP front, 

Meta was hit with hundreds of additional AI-related copyright complaints Monday from authors — around three hundred authors signed on to pursue individual claims over allegations the tech giant took copies of their books and used them to train its large language models without permission.

 The Third Circuit's ruling from earlier this month confirming that 

AI training on a competitor's copyrighted material constituted copyright infringement — and potentially setting landmark precedent

 — continues to reshape litigation strategy across the industry.

The FTC is developing a new rule that could hold social media platforms, digital marketplaces, and search engines accountable for enabling and amplifying impersonation fraud. FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson and Bureau of Consumer Protection Director Christopher Mufarrige argue that technology platforms have the tools to detect fraudulent advertisers and prevent scams, but lack sufficient financial incentives to act.

 

The FTC is requesting public comments on requirements that would encourage platforms to vet advertisers, identify fraudulent activity, and prevent impersonation scams.

 This is notable for a media holding company because it signals the FTC's appetite to move platform liability further upstream into ad-serving decisions. [DEEP DIVE]

The FTC's LiveRamp order is the most important ad-tech development in the last six months for any media publisher that runs programmatic advertising — and that almost certainly includes every Firecrown brand that has display inventory.

Let me lay out the facts first, then the risk analysis, then three concrete actions.

LiveRamp is the dominant data onboarding and identity resolution infrastructure player in the American digital advertising market. When an advertiser wants to match their customer database against publisher audiences, that data movement often flows through LiveRamp's Authenticated Traffic Solution or its Clean Room products. Publishers, agencies, DSPs, and data buyers rely on it as what the market calls a neutral pipe. 

LiveRamp sells data connectivity services that help advertisers move the data behind digital ad campaigns securely and confidentially. Publicis plans and buys media for brands, so its rivals' clients rely on a tool it is about to own.



The FTC's core concern was exactly what you would expect: 

the deal would likely result in Publicis having the ability and incentive to foreclose customers of competing media agency service providers by using its control of LiveRamp to push customers to switch their media agency business to Publicis even if those customers otherwise prefer competing media agencies.



The consent order is a behavioral remedy — no divestiture. 

Under the order, LiveRamp must continue serving clients of rival agencies on neutral terms, Publicis is walled off from LiveRamp customer data, and an outside auditor checks compliance every six months for the first two years after closing.

 

The order is a behavioral fix with no divestiture. It is lighter than the remedy a US judge imposed on Google's ad tech business, which got a monitor for six years.



There is a second wrinkle that received less coverage but matters considerably for publishers. 

The order lands as Apple shuts identity tools like LiveRamp's out of Safari. Apple's WebKit team published its reasoning late on Monday for blocking what it calls identity resolution infrastructure. LiveRamp's domains are among those blocked, along with The Trade Desk, ID5, Permutive, and Audigent.



So you have the following simultaneous conditions. LiveRamp is being absorbed by a competitor to agencies whose publisher clients depend on it. The behavioral remedy ensures access on paper, but paper access and commercial neutrality are not the same thing. Meanwhile, Apple is separately degrading the technical pipe that LiveRamp and its competitors use to resolve identity in Safari, which controls a substantial share of US mobile and desktop browser traffic. The market is being squeezed from two directions at once: ownership concentration at the infrastructure layer and browser-level elimination of identity signals at the client layer.

For a publisher operating brands with meaningful programmatic inventory — FreightWaves, FLYING, Trains.com, Boating and Sailing — the revenue exposure here is real. The addressable audience shrinks as identity resolution degrades. The neutrality of the remaining infrastructure is now under contractual management by the FTC rather than by market competition.

The Spacestore e-commerce dimension is slightly different but still present. Retargeting, audience extension, and prospecting campaigns sold by or to Spacestore use the same infrastructure. If the identity layer erodes, conversion efficiency falls.

Three concrete actions.

First, audit your LiveRamp contractual relationships now — not in six months. The consent order is in a thirty-day public comment period before it becomes final. 

Publicis said the undertakings reflected commitments it had proposed proactively and reiterated that it expected the acquisition to close before the end of 2026.

 Before close happens, understand which of your data flows run through LiveRamp services, and document what the contractual fallback position is if pricing, terms, or access changes. Do not assume the FTC order eliminates the risk; it creates an enforcement mechanism, which is different.

Second, brief your commercial team on the Apple Safari identity block. This is not a future risk. 

Apple's WebKit team published its reasoning for blocking identity resolution infrastructure, and LiveRamp's domains are among those blocked.

 Safari audience extension products sold to advertisers by your brands may already be delivering less than contracted. That is both a revenue risk and a potential client dispute.

Third, use this as the internal catalyst to accelerate first-party data strategy. The ICO enforcement data and the California SB 923 expansion both point in the same direction as the ad-tech market structure: first-party, consented, deterministic data is becoming both the legal standard and the commercial necessity. Firecrown's audience segments across freight, aviation, marine, and enthusiast verticals are professionally defined and highly specific. That is a significant addressable asset in a world where third-party identity infrastructure is under structural pressure from regulators and browsers simultaneously.
