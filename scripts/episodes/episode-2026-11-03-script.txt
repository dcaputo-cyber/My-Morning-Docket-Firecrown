[DEEP DIVE]

Now the deep dive. The subject is the OpenAI consolidated copyright litigation and what its current posture means for a media holding company that is, simultaneously, a potential plaintiff class member, a content licensor, and a business that may want to use AI tools trained on text.

Here is where the case stands as of this morning. 

The parties filed competing summary judgment motions on September fourth, twenty twenty-six.

 

Opposition briefs were filed October twenty-third. Fact and expert discovery closed in the summer of twenty twenty-six.

 The court extended the deadline for amicus briefs to October thirtieth, and what arrived at that deadline changed the shape of the argument considerably.



The Computer and Communications Industry Association co-filed a joint amicus brief along with NetChoice and TechNet, arguing that the use of copyrighted material for AI training qualifies as permissible fair use under the Copyright Act and the general purposes of copyright as established in the Constitution.

 

The brief argues that the use of material for AI is highly transformative because it identifies patterns and enables entirely new technology tools. The amici also dismissed concerns about market dilution — the idea that AI creations could flood a market and decrease value — as an unrecognized and unrealistic theory of harm.





The excerpts attributed to those filers argue that AI training serves a different purpose from the underlying works, does not provide substitute access to copyrighted material, and does not create the kind of market harm copyright law is designed to prevent. The United States government is quoted as saying that narrowing fair-use doctrine to exclude large language model training would be inconsistent with copyright principles and could hinder scientific and economic progress.



But the brief that should most interest a media holding company GC came from Wikimedia. 

The Wikimedia Foundation asked a federal judge presiding over the consolidated copyright litigation against OpenAI to divide fair-use questions between model training and real-time generation. The nonprofit behind Wikipedia argued that pre-training artificial intelligence models on copyrighted text is likely fair use, but that retrieval-augmented generation features in chatbots are not.

 Read that again: training is likely fair use; real-time retrieval is not. 

The organization drew a sharp contrast with retrieval-augmented generation, or RAG, in which a chatbot copies material from external websites to answer user prompts. Wikimedia wrote that while the pre-training of ChatGPT's general language model was fair use, OpenAI's use of copies in ChatGPT's RAG feature is not fair, arguing that systems copying and delivering excerpts to users must respect publishers' licensing terms.



This distinction is not academic. It is the licensing theory on which the next generation of publisher AI deals will rest. If a court adopts the Wikimedia framework — training is fair use, RAG retrieval requires a license — then publishers like FreightWaves, FLYING, and Trains.com have a real licensing claim against any AI service that ingests and then serves up their content in answer to user queries. The content does not have to be reproduced verbatim. The question is whether the retrieval step substitutes for a visit to the publisher.



The source material also reflects a broader split in the litigation landscape, with some courts and analyses treating training, source-copy acquisition, retention and output distribution as separate copyright issues rather than a single category.

 That atomization of the question is what makes this case so important. If the court treats each stage of the AI pipeline separately, Firecrown may have claims in some stages and not others, depending entirely on how its content ended up in a training set and whether it can be retrieved by a RAG system.



A consortium of nineteen affiliated papers sued OpenAI and Microsoft on October eighth, twenty twenty-six, seeking more than two hundred and fifty million dollars, and that case has been paused by agreement until the pending summary judgment motions are decided.

 That pause is a tell. It means the industry is watching this case as a price-setter.

What should you do now as general counsel? Three concrete actions.

One: inventory your content. Identify which FreightWaves, FLYING, Trains.com, Boating and Sailing, and Spacestore content is publicly accessible and therefore crawlable. Cross-reference that against your robots.txt files. OpenAI announced last week that it would honor shared robots.txt AI training signals — that is a low-friction way to assert a preference without litigation, but it must actually be implemented and monitored.

Two: review any AI vendor agreements you have in place. If Firecrown uses any AI tool that ingests your internal editorial content or customer data for training purposes, the agreement should have an explicit prohibition on using Firecrown data to train external models and a warranty that the vendor's training data is lawfully licensed. The Wikimedia brief's RAG theory means even query-time retrieval may require a license — which in turn means your AI vendor's indemnification obligations matter.

Three: consider whether Firecrown has affirmative licensing claims that are worth asserting now, before this case settles and before the industry establishes a licensing rate. The New York Times and the nineteen-paper consortium both moved before the litigation resolved. The reply briefs are due November twentieth. The window for publishers to join the narrative, whether by filing, licensing discussions, or public comment, is open but narrowing.

the SONAR Truckload Rejection Index spiked to an unexpected fourteen percent in mid-October — a significant development outside normal seasonal patterns. This is not driven by a demand spike, but rather by persistent market imbalance and insufficient supply across the country. Key regions including Los Angeles, Harrisburg, and Atlanta are showing unusual disruptive activity, with spot rates rising in critical areas.

 Context for why this matters: 

over the three years ending October twenty twenty-six, the SONAR Truckload Volume Index is up nine percent, while the Truckload Rejection Index is up two hundred and sixty-eight percent.

 Volume has barely moved. Capacity has not returned. 

Shippers who have reset truckload contracts over the past several months are paying about thirteen percent more than the rates they replaced. That is the reading from SONAR's new Contract Rate Change Direction index, which stood at thirteen-point-eleven percent on October twenty-second, up from three-point-five-three percent a year earlier.



SONAR also launched new routing guide data this week. 

SONAR released three new truckload dataset groups that measure what shippers pay their transportation providers — brokers and carriers — as freight moves through a routing guide and as contract rates reset.

 

As of October twentieth, loads accepted on the second tender cost shippers a median twenty-two percent more all-in than first-tender loads.
