The decision marks the first federal appellate ruling on fair use in AI training, but the reach of the opinion is limited because the AI technology at the heart of the case was itself relatively limited — ROSS's product used training data to identify and return existing judicial passages rather than generate new expression.

[DEEP DIVE]

The Thomson Reuters and Ross Intelligence decision from the Third Circuit is worth ten minutes of your time because of what it says, what it does not say, and what it signals for the next twelve months of AI copyright litigation.

Start with the facts. 

ROSS partnered with a company named LegalEASE to create roughly twenty-five thousand training memos that would be used to build its AI legal research model. The lawyers producing those memos were told to access Westlaw but not to copy-paste from it. However, twenty-two hundred forty-three of Thomson Reuters' headnotes were copied regardless.



The Third Circuit's analysis proceeded through the four fair use factors. 

On the first factor — the purpose and character of the challenged use — the court found it favored Thomson Reuters. ROSS's use of the headnotes was only minimally transformative because its AI product used the headnotes in essentially the same way that Westlaw was using them: to improve a legal research service.

 

Relying on the Supreme Court's twenty twenty-three Warhol versus Goldsmith decision, the court found that ROSS put the work to the same use as Westlaw and therefore the use was not transformative because it did not have a further purpose or a different character.



On the fourth factor — market harm — the court identified two distinct markets that ROSS harmed. 

The existing legal research market, where ROSS's AI product was designed to compete directly with Westlaw, and a developing market for licensing headnotes as AI training data.

 That second market finding is the most consequential sentence in this opinion for you as a media company. The court has now validated, at the appellate level, the existence of a licensing market for content used as AI training data. When a publisher negotiates a training data licensing deal with a foundation model company, that deal is no longer commercially eccentric. It is legally grounded.

Now for the limits of the ruling, and they matter. 

The Third Circuit distinguished ROSS Intelligence's AI product from systems that can generate original expression, leaving the central generative-AI debate unresolved.

 The court explicitly said this is an ordinary copyright case — not a referendum on the future of AI. 

As Judge Montgomery-Reeves wrote: the undisputed evidence reflects that Thomson Reuters' materials possess a creative spark and ROSS aspired to be a direct competitor by using them for a highly similar purpose. The court held Thomson Reuters' materials are copyrightable and ROSS's use was not fair.



The minimum transformation test and the direct-competitor market-harm finding will not automatically apply to a generative AI company that trained on web-scraped journalism and produces summaries, synthetic articles, or chatbot answers. Those are different fact patterns. But the legal logic is directionally unfavorable for the AI developers. When a foundation model company trains on your editorial content and then its product competes with your subscription offering — whether by surfacing answers instead of driving clicks, or by generating aviation content that competes with FLYING, or freight market intelligence that competes with FreightWaves — you now have an appellate court that has at minimum recognized the architecture of that harm claim.

The DOJ's opposing position in the OpenAI MDL complicates the picture further. 

Rather than leaving the AI training question to a broad factual spectrum, the government urges a practical rule: courts should separate internal training from public outputs and should not treat the mere use of copyrighted text to train a large language model as infringement absent a distinct use that substitutes for protected expression.

 The government's brief and the Third Circuit's decision are not necessarily incompatible — they concern different AI architectures — but they create a litigation landscape where outcomes are fact-specific, circuit-specific, and likely to diverge until the Supreme Court intervenes. 

The next major AI fair use hearing is Concord versus Anthropic, currently scheduled for October twenty twenty-six.

The Third Circuit specifically validated copyright in curated editorial expression. Map that exposure now rather than in response to a complaint.

Second, formalize your licensing posture. The court's recognition of an emerging AI training data licensing market means a licensing framework is more enforceable today than it was thirty days ago. If your brands' terms of service do not already prohibit scraping for AI training, update them. where the court ruled AI training on copyrighted books constitutes fair use, but storing pirated copies does not, leading to a one point five billion dollar settlement with an estimated payout of roughly three thousand dollars per work
