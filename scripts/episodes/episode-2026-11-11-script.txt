Second item. The FTC and Amazon on ad auction pricing. On November fifth, the FTC and twenty-two state attorneys general unsealed an amended complaint against Amazon in federal court in Seattle. 

The complaint alleges Amazon raised advertising prices by as much as fifty percent above auction results on ordinary days and as much as two hundred fifty percent on major shopping days like Prime Day and Black Friday.

 

The complaint alleges the practices affected Amazon's Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Sponsored Display ads, and estimates that Amazon took more than twenty billion dollars from approximately one-point-two million advertising customers.

 The legal theory is that Amazon operates what it represents to advertisers as a second-price auction — where the winner pays just enough to beat the next-highest bidder — but allegedly overrode those results with undisclosed pricing adjustments. 

According to the FTC's original August complaint, Amazon introduced undisclosed pricing mechanisms that could increase the amount advertisers paid beyond the price produced by the advertised auction process, and Amazon representatives attributed higher charges to increased competition, even when the company's undisclosed pricing adjustments were allegedly responsible.

 Amazon has denied the allegations. For any media company running advertising across Amazon's properties, this complaint is a document worth reading — not just because of what it says about Amazon, but because it articulates a fraud theory around undisclosed auction modifications that regulators could apply elsewhere.

Craig Fuller offered a broader structural read at Optym's Convergence 2026 conference in Frisco last week, and it deserves attention beyond the rate numbers. 

Small carriers and brokers waiting for Washington to ease up on trucking enforcement should not expect relief, Fuller said, because the industry's largest fleets now want the crackdown.

 

He tied together the federal push against non-domiciled commercial driver's licenses, a Supreme Court ruling that opened brokers to negligent-hiring suits, and a substantial jury verdict against a major freight company.
