[HEADLINE ROUNDUP]

First. CalPrivacy's opt-out rulemaking kicks into formal gear. At its November fifth and sixth board meeting, the California Privacy Protection Agency voted unanimously to begin formal rulemaking on amendments to the CCPA regulations. The proposed amendments address how businesses must honor opt-out preference signals and how they report on consumer requests. Staff also approved preliminary recommendations for a separate rulemaking covering the content of privacy notices and disclosures, including a proposed requirement that privacy policies expressly state whether the business sold or shared personal information during the preceding twelve months. Separately, the agency has now published what trackers count as nineteen enforcement actions totaling more than thirty-two million dollars in penalties. The most recent additions: Polaris Industries, fined just over nine hundred ten thousand dollars on November nineteenth after a joint investigation with the Minnesota Attorney General — the first publicly disclosed multi-state coordinated action under the Consortium of Privacy Regulators — and the Hertz Corporation, fined one million five hundred fifty thousand dollars two days earlier. Both cases turned on failure to honor the Global Privacy Control signal and prohibited sales after consumers had opted out. For Firecrown's media brands, the enforcement pattern is unambiguous: if any of your properties carry cross-context behavioral advertising and your tech stack does not fully recognize GPC, you are within the enforcement perimeter that CalPrivacy has drawn.

Second. The FTC and twenty-two states unseal the Amazon ads auction-rigging complaint. On November fifth, the FTC announced a largely unredacted amended complaint against Amazon alleging the company secretly raised advertising prices by as much as fifty percent above auction results on ordinary days and by as much as two hundred fifty percent on major shopping days like Prime Day and Black Friday. The complaint — originally filed in August against Amazon in federal court in the Western District of Washington — alleges Amazon told advertisers its sponsored products auctions ran on a generalized second-price model, while internally adding a mechanism that effectively converted them to first-price auctions close to eighty percent of the time. The government alleges the scheme extracted more than twenty billion dollars from approximately one-point-two million advertising customers. Amazon has rejected the allegations and says regulators misunderstand the auction's operation. The significance for a media holding company: if you are running any campaigns on Amazon's ad network for Spacestore or any of the Firecrown brands, your historical cost-per-click data may not reflect what you were actually paying relative to competing bids. This is a case worth watching from both a vendor-contract and a budget-audit perspective.

Third. The FTC and DOJ raise the temperature on ad-tech platform accountability. At the National Advertising Division's annual conference this autumn, FTC Deputy Director Katherine White identified advertising optimization platforms — alongside payment processors and social media — as a priority enforcement target for platforms that enable or enhance fraud. The agency separately published an advance notice of proposed rulemaking in October seeking comment on whether ad-optimization services offered by digital platforms facilitate impersonation scams, and the comment window closes November thirtieth. That is six days from today. If Firecrown or any of its brands operate advertising inventory, the question the FTC is explicitly asking — whether platforms should be required to screen advertisers and prevent impersonation ads — is relevant to every publisher running programmatic. The Publicis acquisition of LiveRamp closed November second after the FTC conditioned it on a consent order requiring Publicis to provide non-discriminatory access to LiveRamp's data connectivity services to all clients, including competitors, with firewalls protecting rival data, for five years. The FTC converted Publicis's voluntary statements into enforceable obligations backed by an independent auditor. For Firecrown's ad operations team, LiveRamp's neutrality obligations are now a contract right, not a promise.

Fourth. The DOJ's AI training brief draws a congressional reaction. As a bridge to our deep dive: a group of House Democrats sent a letter to the Department of Justice on November nineteenth expressing alarm that the DOJ had filed a statement of interest in the OpenAI copyright MDL without consulting the Copyright Office or the Patent and Trademark Office. The DOJ's September filing argued that training large language models on copyrighted text is fair use — describing the use as highly transformative and warning that requiring licensing would favor only the largest technology companies while threatening national security objectives. The House Democrats said the filing raises, in their words, serious questions about potential bias. That political friction now surrounds a summary judgment record on which the last reply briefs were due November twentieth, making a ruling possible in the first quarter of twenty twenty-seven.

Fifth. The SEC proposes the most significant executive compensation disclosure overhaul since two thousand six. On November thirteenth, the SEC issued a five-hundred-forty-page proposing release amending Item four-oh-two of Regulation S-K. The proposal would move from prescriptive line-item disclosure to a more principles-based standard. Key changes include reducing the number of named executive officers from five to two in baseline disclosure, eliminating the pay-versus-performance table in its current form, eliminating the pension benefits and nonqualified deferred compensation tables, raising the perquisite disclosure threshold from ten thousand to one hundred fifty thousand dollars per executive, and replacing the quantitative hypothetical termination and change-in-control payment table with a qualitative narrative. The SEC estimates average annual compliance cost savings of approximately two hundred forty-four thousand dollars per company. If finalized in twenty twenty-seven, mandatory compliance would begin with fiscal year twenty twenty-eight compensation reported in proxy filings in twenty twenty-nine. For Firecrown as a private company, Item four-oh-two does not apply directly, but Darren, any debt covenant, lender package, or acquisition diligence process that benchmarks you against public-company disclosure standards will feel this shift. Watch the comment period.

Sixth. The UK Information Commission — the former ICO — is publishing updated guidance in real time under the Data Use and Access Act. On September thirtieth, the ICO formally became the Information Commission under the Data Use and Access Act two thousand twenty-five, a board-led body replacing the single office of the Information Commissioner. As of this week, the regulator is rolling out updated guidance across its entire website to reflect DUAA amendments that came into force in stages from February through September of this year. The ICO updated its law enforcement processing guidance on November ninth. The critical operating point for Firecrown's UK-touching brands: the new enforcement powers introduced by the DUAA apply only to conduct occurring after February fifth, twenty twenty-six, but those powers are materially stronger — the regulator can now compel interviews and answers under new investigatory authority. Cookie consent reforms under PECR remain pending; no statutory instrument had been laid as of this week. That gap is real — do not assume cookie banners need to change yet — but the broader enforcement posture of the new commission is demonstrably more assertive.

[DEEP DIVE]

The OpenAI copyright MDL and what the DOJ brief means for a media publisher that is also an AI user.

Take ten minutes with this one, because it has two sides for Firecrown and they pull in opposite directions.

Here is the procedural state of play. In re: OpenAI, Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation, MDL three one four three in the Southern District of New York, consolidates copyright claims brought by The New York Times and other news publishers, Ziff Davis, and a class of authors including the Authors Guild, all against OpenAI and Microsoft. Both sides filed competing summary judgment motions on September fourth asking Judge Sidney Stein to decide the core fair use question without a trial. Opposition briefs came in October twenty-third, and reply briefs were due November twentieth — meaning the record is now complete. No ruling has been issued and no trial date set. This is the first case in New York to reach this stage, the first involving OpenAI specifically, and the first consolidating both news publishers and book authors.

The government's position, filed September first by the Department of Justice, is that training a large language model on copyrighted text is fair use and that ruling otherwise would, quote, be problematic and legally incorrect. The DOJ argues the training use is highly transformative — it makes different functional use of text to teach a model how language works, rather than substituting for the original work in any reader's market. On the fourth factor of fair use analysis, market harm, the government argues the legally relevant question is not whether AI causes some loss of sales but whether training creates significant substitutive competition for the copyrighted works themselves. The DOJ adds a national security frame: requiring licenses for training data would impose costs that only the largest technology companies could absorb, potentially creating an oligopoly and disadvantaging the United States relative to foreign AI developers.

Now, the House Democrats' letter of November nineteenth argues that the DOJ's filing was made without consulting the Copyright Office, which has historically treated fair use for generative AI training as a fact-specific, case-by-case inquiry. That procedural concern does not change the legal argument, but it signals that the DOJ's position is politically contested even within the executive branch.

Here is the tension for Firecrown. On one side of the ledger, FreightWaves is original journalistic and analytical content. FLYING, Boating and Sailing, Trains dot com — these are publications with archives worth protecting. If the MDL produces a ruling that training on published text is fair use with no licensing requirement, the economic model of content licensing for AI becomes very difficult. Anthropic just struck its first publisher licensing deal with the Associated Press; the New York Times has an active licensing program. The DOJ's argument that large publishers would become subsidy-seekers while indie publishers get shut out reads, to a Firecrown GC, as a description of competitive disadvantage for smaller specialized publishers, not protection.

On the other side of the ledger, Firecrown brands rely on FreightWaves's data-and-media hybrid model and SONAR's freight intelligence platform. Those products are increasingly AI-augmented. AI tools trained on broad text corpora — including logistics, aviation, and marine content — make the FreightWaves editorial and analytics product better. A ruling that constricts AI training imposes costs on the AI vendors that power the tools the brands use internally.

Three concrete actions.

One: audit your content licensing posture now, before the ruling. If FreightWaves and other Firecrown properties have not taken a formal position on whether AI crawlers may access their content, the absence of a robots.txt exclusion or terms-of-service restriction could be used as evidence of implied license in the MDL briefing. That is a legal position you want to control, not discover after the fact.

Two: if Firecrown is using any AI-powered tools that require the vendor to process your proprietary content — editorial drafts, SONAR datasets, subscriber data — review those vendor agreements for training data clauses right now. The MDL outcome could directly affect the indemnification value of whatever representations those vendors are making.

Three: register a monitoring protocol for the Eminem publisher case against Suno. Eight Mile Style and Martin Affiliated filed an expanded complaint on November nineteenth adding one hundred eight allegedly infringing AI-generated tracks to their forty-seven-point-seven million dollar lawsuit. That case is testing whether AI music generators that train on copyrighted recordings and then produce commercial output can claim fair use. The analytical framework the Suno court applies will almost certainly be cited by Judge Stein. The music litigation is the canary — watch it.

[GC BENCH]

Legal technology and in-house AI this week.

The headline from Legal Business and Thomson Reuters: a survey of more than two hundred fifty senior in-house lawyers across the UK and Europe published November eleventh found that eighty-two percent of respondents are already implementing generative AI solutions within their teams, and almost eighty percent identified increasing workflow efficiency without adding headcount as a priority. Only five percent wanted to reduce team size. The phrase circulating from the survey is blunt: we now ask the AI first. The Thomson Reuters associate general counsel quoted in the piece described AI as, in her framing, more like a team of colleagues.

The in-house briefing published the week of November sixth noted that Allen and Overy Shearman has committed one-point-one billion dollars to AI — paid substantially in lawyer hours rather than cash — which means that every Big Law firm pricing conversation that GC has with outside counsel now carries an implicit AI efficiency question on the other side. If the firm has committed to AI productivity and is billing on a time basis, the GC's leverage point is to ask what that investment is returning in reduced hours on your matters.

Gartner's finding from the same week is a useful counterweight: ninety-seven percent of organizations cannot cut costs through productivity alone. The implication for legal: AI buys capacity, not headcount reduction. The fifteen chief legal officers surveyed in the same period were uniform on this point. This matters for how Firecrown budgets legal operations — the ROI case for legal AI tools is a capacity case, not a cost-cutting case, and if you present it as headcount reduction internally you will face resistance and misaligned expectations.

Practically: if you are evaluating purpose-built in-house AI platforms — the category includes GC AI, Harvey, Spellbook, CoCounsel, and Legora — the landscape has consolidated enough that published pricing and head-to-head task benchmarks are now available. The price entry points range from five hundred dollars per month for individual in-house seats to enterprise arrangements. The differentiation that matters for a lean GC function at a media holding company is whether the platform handles contract portfolios and regulatory research in a single workflow, rather than requiring separate tools for each.

[INDUSTRY ANGLE]

Freight, aviation, and marine signals through Thanksgiving week.

SONAR's data as reported by FreightWaves on November ninth and tenth paints a market that is tighter than it looks on the surface. The SONAR Tender Volume Index climbed nearly five percent week over week, reaching eleven thousand six hundred — a level last seen in late spring. November's Outbound Tender Volume Index is running twelve percent above the same period last year and roughly three percent above October, which FreightWaves described as the first meaningful demand build heading into the holiday season. At the same time, all-mode tender rejections were sitting at approximately twelve-point-nine-five percent as of November ninth, with van spot rates at three dollars and forty-nine cents per mile and van contract rates touching a fifty-two-week high of two dollars and eighty-five cents. Refrigerated is the standout: reefer rejections at twenty-one percent, more than eight points above the same time last year, driven by Thanksgiving food shipments. Q3 SONAR data confirms this is a supply-driven recovery rather than a demand boom — tender rejections averaged fourteen-point-five percent over the quarter versus five-point-three percent a year earlier, while accepted volume grew only in the single digits.

Craig Fuller, speaking at Optym's Convergence 2026 conference on November second and reported by FreightWaves on November seventh, argued that FMCSA enforcement is now being sustained by the largest carriers, not by regulators. Fuller's framing: the biggest fleets want tighter driver standards because cheap, non-compliant capacity undercut their pricing for years, so they have no interest in Washington easing enforcement. He described trucking as simultaneously facing a security crisis, a safety crisis, and an economic crisis — with freight theft appearing on FreightWaves' daily show every single day. He also noted that brokers, who now handle more than thirty percent of freight volume compared to six percent in the early two-thousands, face their own compliance exposure from the Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II Supreme Court ruling that is reshaping broker liability. For FreightWaves as an editorial and data brand covering this sector, these structural dynamics are the story of the year heading into twenty twenty-seven bid season.

On aviation: technology-driven airfreight demand continues to tighten November capacity. Asia-Pacific-to-US volume rose roughly ten percent in week forty-four, with spot rates from South Korea and Japan exceeding eight dollars per kilogram. Expeditors credited AI hyperscalers and high-value technology customers for record air tonnage. For FLYING and Firecrown's aviation titles, the air cargo economics tell a story that general aviation audiences and business aviation operators should understand — premium cargo is competing for narrowbody belly capacity on exactly the routes that business travelers use.

On marine: the White House extended a Jones Act shipping waiver on November twelfth. CMA CGM reported a volatile third quarter with Middle East shipping disruptions and rerouted trade networks affecting results, though they described the global economy as showing relative resilience. Maersk's November market update noted the extended import peak season beginning to ease but with localized capacity constraints, elevated operating costs, and weather-related disruptions persisting on inland networks. Dry bulk scrapping has fallen to an eighteen-year low because strong freight rates have extended the operational lives of older vessels, according to BIMCO — a data point that matters for Boating and Sailing's coverage of the commercial marine sector.
