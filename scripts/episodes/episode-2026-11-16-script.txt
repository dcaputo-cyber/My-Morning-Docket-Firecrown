[DEEP DIVE]

Today's deep dive: the FTC and twenty-two states versus Amazon's advertising auction system, and what it means for every media company running programmatic or sponsored-content operations.

Let's establish the facts clearly. 

On August thirty-first, 2026, the FTC and twenty-two state attorneys general sued Amazon in the United States District Court for the Western District of Washington over its online search advertising auction practices.

 

The FTC and the states then expanded the case in an updated, largely unredacted complaint filed October twenty-eighth and announced November fifth, which put specific numbers and internal descriptions to the alleged scheme.



Here is the core allegation: 

Amazon is accused of adding undisclosed surcharges to its advertising auctions, pushing prices above the amounts determined by the auction itself.

 

The FTC alleges that Amazon's reserve prices lifted ad prices by as much as two hundred fifty percent above auction results on days like Prime Day and Black Friday.

 The company trained its own advertisers to expect a second-price auction — where you pay the second-highest bid, not your own. 

Amazon's own training materials told advertisers their cost "may actually be lower than your bid, because Amazon Ads uses a second price auction."

 The unredacted complaint alleges that internally Amazon was aware of the disconnect. 

According to the FTC, Amazon acknowledged internally that its price increases led to decreases in the advertiser's return on ad spend that were described as "destroying campaign performance."



Amazon disputes the allegations and has called the case misguided. 

The company says no advertiser pays more than its bid, and the case could take years to litigate.



Why does this matter for a media company general counsel? Three reasons.

First, the contractual question. 

For in-house counsel and compliance teams, the case is a reminder that ad-tech governance is now squarely in the enforcement spotlight. Companies operating digital marketplaces, sponsored-product systems, or bid-based ad tools should expect scrutiny not just of outcomes, but of auction rules, ranking logic, reserve-price decisions, and internal experimentation.

 If you sell advertising inventory through a third-party platform that uses auction mechanics — and virtually every digital publisher does — your contracts need to reflect that you are receiving representations about how that auction works. When those representations are allegedly false, you have a downstream problem: either you passed incorrect information to your own advertisers, or your advertisers suffered losses they will attribute to you.

Second, the revenue accounting question. If the Amazon allegations are proved — again, contested, years away from trial — the question becomes whether publishers who embedded Amazon advertising or affiliate commerce received commissions based on inflated cost-per-click prices. That is an audit question before it becomes a legal question. FreightWaves, FLYING, and the e-commerce properties at Spacestore all carry some form of performance advertising. Now is the time to understand exactly what auction mechanics underlie every revenue stream.

Third, the broader doctrinal signal. 

The case raises doctrinal questions about when platform optimization crosses into exclusionary conduct, what proof is required to show auction manipulation, and how courts will evaluate injury in complex multi-sided digital markets.

 The FTC has previously gone after Cox Media Group this year for allegedly claiming that smart speakers could be used to target ads based on overheard audio. 

That action resulted in an eight-hundred-eighty thousand dollar settlement with Cox Media Group in May 2026.

 The pattern is consistent: ad-tech claims that cannot be substantiated, auction mechanics that do not function as represented, and attribution that cannot be verified are all in the enforcement crosshairs.

Here are three concrete actions for Darren, as general counsel at Firecrown.

Action one. Pull your platform agreements with any auction-based ad network — Google Ad Manager, any Amazon publisher services, any SSP — and require that your counterparties provide written representations that auction mechanics function as described in their standard documentation. That is not an unusual ask. It is now a defensible compliance posture. Build it into your next contract renewal cycle.

Action two. Commission an internal audit of how your properties describe ad pricing to direct advertisers. If you are running a Firecrown-branded advertising product that uses programmatic auction mechanics underneath, your media kit needs to accurately describe what buyers are actually getting. The FTC's attention to auction misrepresentation is not confined to Amazon. It applies to any party making representations about how digital ad pricing is determined.

Action three. Document your internal ad product governance. If you have anyone at FreightWaves or any Firecrown property who has influence over reservation prices, floor prices, or yield management settings, that person should be operating under a written policy with periodic review. When a regulator subpoenas internal communications about pricing decisions — as happened with Amazon — you want those decisions to look considered, not improvised.
