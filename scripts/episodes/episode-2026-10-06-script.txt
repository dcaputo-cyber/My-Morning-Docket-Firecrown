The FTC took a first step toward crafting regulations that could require online platforms to take steps to combat fraudulent ads by scammers who impersonate businesses and government agencies.

 

The agency said in the notice that these impersonation scams have been amplified by search engine, social media, and other digital marketplace platforms that profit from optimizing online ads for third parties, regardless of whether those third parties are legitimate.

[DEEP DIVE]

Now to the ruling that came down just one week ago and that I want you to think about carefully, because it is the first federal appellate decision in the United States on whether training an AI model on copyrighted material can constitute fair use. The short answer from the Third Circuit is: not in this case, and the reasoning matters even where the holding technically does not apply.

The case is Thomson Reuters versus ROSS Intelligence. 

On September 29, 2026, a three-judge Third Circuit panel affirmed a 2025 lower court decision in the matter, a landmark copyright dispute concerning artificial intelligence, the fair use defense, and legal search tools.

 

ROSS Intelligence used Westlaw headnotes to train an AI product that returned passages from existing judicial opinions and was designed to compete directly with Westlaw. The court concluded that the use was highly commercial and only minimally transformative. It also found harm to both Westlaw's existing legal-research market and a developing market for licensing headnotes as AI-training data.





The Third Circuit affirmed that ROSS infringed more than two thousand copyrighted headnote summaries of U.S. case law from Westlaw, and also affirmed the district court's denial of ROSS's fair use defense, marking the first major ruling by a U.S. circuit court on the applicability of fair use defenses to training AI models on copyrighted content.



That last sentence is the headline. The first circuit-level ruling in the country on AI training and fair use went against the AI developer.

Now, before you call outside counsel to panic about every AI vendor contract in the Firecrown stack, the Third Circuit was careful to draw a box around its holding. 

The Third Circuit's decision marks the first federal appellate ruling on fair use in AI training. But the reach of the opinion is limited because the AI technology at the heart of the case was itself relatively limited: ROSS Intelligence's AI product used training data to identify and return existing judicial passages rather than generate new expression.

 

When judged against the fair use framework established by the Supreme Court in the 2023 Andy Warhol case, the non-generative legal research AI platform developed by ROSS made a product competing with Westlaw and not a transformative use.



So the court explicitly distinguished ROSS's non-generative tool from a large language model that generates novel text. That is meaningful. But here is why the ruling still matters for you specifically.

First, the market harm analysis. 

The court found harm to both Westlaw's existing legal-research market and a developing market for licensing headnotes as AI-training data.

 That second part — a developing market for licensing training data — is language that any plaintiff's counsel will now cite in cases involving publishers. If courts recognize that AI training data constitutes an emerging licensing market, then the argument that scraping your editorial content for AI training is not market-harmful becomes much harder to sustain.

Second, the commercial nature of the use. The court placed significant weight on the fact that ROSS copied Westlaw's material not to comment on it, not to criticize it, not to teach about it, but to build a competing commercial product. 

A judicial consensus is developing that training a general-purpose AI model is highly transformative, a factor favoring the finding of fair use

 — that's the contrasting view from Morrison Foerster's analysis of the generative AI cases. But the key word there is general-purpose. The moment an AI vendor is using your specific journalistic content, your SONAR-derived freight data analysis, your FLYING editorial voice, to build a product that competes with your subscription offering, the commercial and competitive analysis tilts toward infringement, not toward fair use.

Third, what is happening in parallel. 

On July 14th, a coalition of publishers and authors — including Hachette, Cengage, Elsevier, and novelist Scott Turow — filed a class action lawsuit against Google, alleging that the company used their copyrighted works to train its Gemini models without permission, and that Google intentionally removed or altered copyright management information embedded in the works to obscure the fact that Gemini had been trained on the material.

 That copyright management information angle — the stripping of metadata — is a separate DMCA theory that runs parallel to the fair use question, and it is gaining traction. 

There is also a complaint in the Southern District of New York against Runway AI for alleged violations of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, arising from allegations of unlawfully circumventing technological measures to access and scrape copyrighted videos from YouTube.



So the landscape as of today, October 6th, looks like this. The Third Circuit has handed publishers a circuit-level precedent they can point to. Courts deciding generative AI cases are more likely to find fair use for transformative general-purpose models, but that protection has limits. And the Anthropic settlement — 

Anthropic's practice of retaining pirated digital copies of books was not excused by the fair use finding. That distinction proved expensive.

 How the training data was sourced and stored matters independently of whether the training itself qualifies as fair use.

Action one: conduct an audit of the AI tools your editorial teams are already using. Are any of those tools trained on your own archives or on third-party content scraped without license? The ROSS ruling makes clear that commercial purpose and competitive substitution are the key variables. Action two: review your own content's licensing posture. If any of your brands are in Google's AI Contribution Pilot, or if you are considering it, the opaque payment calculation reported this week should give you pause about waiving stronger licensing rights for sub-threshold revenue. The larger publishers declining to participate and seeking direct licensing deals have the right instinct. You are smaller than News Corp, but you have specialized vertical content that is disproportionately valuable to AI training in freight, aviation, and marine. That asymmetry should drive your negotiating strategy.

Action three: update your terms of service and robots.txt governance across all Firecrown brands. In the Google Gemini case, the allegation involves removal of copyright management information. Make sure your content carries embedded metadata, bylines, publication dates, and rights statements that survive scraping. Courts are beginning to treat CMI stripping as an independent violation, and your evidentiary position improves considerably if your original content carried that information before it was removed.
