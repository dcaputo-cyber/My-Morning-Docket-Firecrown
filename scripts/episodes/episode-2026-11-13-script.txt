First. CalPrivacy doubles down on data broker enforcement. The California Privacy Protection Agency, now formally rebranded CalPrivacy, spent the third quarter of twenty twenty-six issuing what may be its most consequential enforcement action yet. 

In its first enforcement under both the CCPA and the Delete Act, CalPrivacy issued a decision alleging both failure to timely register as a data broker under the Delete Act and requiring consumers to provide sensitive personal information, including partial Social Security numbers, before allowing them to exercise their right to opt out of sales.

 That is a compound violation — one for the registration failure, one for the friction in the rights mechanism itself. 

California regulators have announced seventeen point one million dollars in CCPA fines so far in twenty twenty-six, more than double the seven point nine million from every earlier year combined.

 For media companies: Firecrown's brands run newsletters, e-commerce through Spacestore, and audience data operations across multiple platforms. Second. The FTC and twenty-two states unseal the Amazon ads complaint. This one is significant for every company that buys search advertising. 

The FTC and twenty-two states recently filed an updated version of their complaint against Amazon with unredacted details, alleging that Amazon secretly raises advertising prices by as much as fifty percent above auction results on ordinary days and by as much as two hundred and fifty percent on major shopping days like Prime Day or Black Friday.

 

When advertisers facing prices inflated by Amazon's hidden surcharges asked if something had changed with respect to the second-price auction process, Amazon falsely reassured them the price increases were the result of increased competition, according to the complaint.

 Amazon disputes all of it. 

Amazon has denied the allegations, saying the complaint cites no evidence of consumer price increases, that advertisers never pay more than their bid, and that its ranking saved advertisers more than eight billion dollars from twenty twenty-one to twenty twenty-five.

 The legal theory here is deception — not antitrust. That distinction matters for how damages could be calculated. If you are spending on sponsored product or display placements anywhere in the Amazon ecosystem for Spacestore, your contracts and insertion orders deserve a second read.

Third. The FTC also moves on ad platform impersonation liability. Separate from the Amazon case, 

the Federal Trade Commission is considering whether digital platforms should be liable when their advertising tools create, target, or optimize advertisements that impersonate governments or businesses.

 

The existing Impersonation Rule prohibits the impersonation of government agencies and businesses, and the FTC is currently considering updates to the rule or a new rule to increase accountability for social media, search engines, and other digital marketplace platforms that further impersonation scams.

 

The public comment deadline is November thirtieth, twenty twenty-six.

 For a media holding company whose brands appear in search results and social feeds, this proposed rulemaking is worth watching — both because your brands could be impersonated and because the FTC's ad-optimization liability theory could affect the platforms on which you spend.

[DEEP DIVE]

The Amazon ads auction-rigging case as a media company legal audit. Let me spend some time unpacking why this case deserves more than a headline read for Firecrown's legal function.

The basic allegation is this. Amazon operates what it calls a second-price auction for Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands, and Sponsored Display placements. In a true second-price auction, the winner pays just one cent more than the next-highest bidder. 

Amazon says ad prices are set by that second-price auction, where the winner pays just enough to beat the next-highest bidder. The complaint alleges Amazon secretly overrode the auction results and charged more.

 

The complaint estimates that Amazon took more than twenty billion dollars from about one point two million advertising customers.

 Those are the FTC's allegations, which Amazon disputes. But the underlying mechanics described — a hidden reserve price, described in the complaint as a "soft reserve," applied to meet internal revenue targets — matter regardless of how the case resolves.

Why does this matter to a media holding company? Two angles. The first is as an advertiser. Spacestore runs performance marketing. FreightWaves and other Firecrown properties may run sponsored content or reader acquisition campaigns. If you are buying Sponsored Products or Display placements on Amazon, the contract language you agreed to almost certainly describes a second-price auction. 

The FTC's updated allegations connect Amazon's advertising pricing decisions to internal financial objectives. In twenty twenty-three, company executives discussed generating an additional forty million dollars to help meet corporate revenue targets, according to the complaint.

 That is the kind of internal document that tends to move litigation. The practical implication: preserve your campaign records, your cost-per-click data, and any communications with Amazon account managers where you asked about pricing changes. If a class action follows the FTC case — and they typically do — your records are your evidence.

The second angle is as a publisher watching what this case does to programmatic norms more broadly. The FTC's theory does not live only inside Amazon's walled garden. The advance notice of proposed rulemaking on ad-optimization platform liability — which has a comment deadline of November thirtieth — asks whether the same accountability logic should extend to any digital marketplace platform whose tools optimize and target ads. 

The agency's September request for public comment asks whether to amend the rule or develop a new rule addressing digital platforms' role in promoting impersonation scams through ad-optimization tools and services. That potential rulemaking remains under consideration and does not itself impose new obligations on platforms.

 For media companies that operate programmatic supply, the trajectory of FTC reasoning here — from deception in the buy-side to liability for optimization on the platform side — is worth watching as a governance matter, not just a compliance one.

The deeper legal lesson from this week's unsealing is about transparency obligations in advertising contracts. 

When advertisers asked why costs jumped, the complaint alleges Amazon's public-relations and legal teams approved a reply blaming shopper and advertiser engagement, an explanation the complaint calls false. The FTC's release says Amazon falsely reassured advertisers.

 Regardless of the case outcome, the regulatory expectation that is crystallizing across the FTC's advertising enforcement work in twenty twenty-six is this: when a buyer asks you a direct question about your pricing mechanism, an incomplete or misleading answer creates legal exposure. That standard applies to media companies selling advertising inventory too.

Three actions for Firecrown's legal function coming out of this deep dive.

Action one: conduct a brief audit of advertising insertion orders and platform terms for any commitment to a described auction mechanism or pricing floor. If the contractual description diverges from actual billing behavior — on either the buy or the sell side — that is your first exposure point.

Action two: on the buy side, particularly for Spacestore's performance marketing, document your cost-per-click baseline from the past twelve months. If the Amazon case proceeds to a class action, advertiser standing will likely require a showing of measurable overpayment, and clean records now reduce your litigation burden later.

Action three: register a comment or instruct outside counsel to monitor the FTC's advance notice of proposed rulemaking on ad-platform liability, with the November thirtieth deadline. Even if you do not file, the comment record will shape the eventual rule, and knowing what your peers and competitors are arguing gives you a strategic read on where platform obligations are heading.
