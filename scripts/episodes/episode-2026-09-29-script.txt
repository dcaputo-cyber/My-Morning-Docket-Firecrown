Those provisions carry out the governance reform Parliament wrote into the Data Use and Access Act 2025: replacing the office of Information Commissioner, a corporation sole held by one person, with the Information Commission, a body corporate with collective decision-making.

 

Unlike the ICO, which was a corporation sole with authority vested in the Information Commissioner personally, the Information Commission will be a body corporate governed by a board comprising a Chair, a CEO, and seven non-executive members. Here is the structural picture. 

Judge Sidney Stein is overseeing multidistrict proceedings that consolidate several generative AI training disputes into a single courtroom. His rulings on fair use thresholds could set binding precedent for how copyright law applies to machine learning.

 Inside that MDL, you have at minimum three distinct populations of plaintiff: news publishers led by the Times, book authors and trade publishers, and general periodicals. Firecrown sits in the general periodicals universe, but FreightWaves — as a data and journalism hybrid with proprietary content — may have specific characteristics worth examining separately.

The DOJ's September 1st filing makes a three-part argument. First, that LLM training is transformative because the purpose of copying — building an intelligent, interactive model — differs in kind from the purpose of the copied work. Second, that national security and AI competitiveness justify a permissive fair-use finding. Third, and critically, that its fair-use argument applies only to the training stage and deliberately does not cover acquisition, storage, or output questions. 

The DOJ filing materially changes the arguments before the court by urging a fair-use view of training-stage copying and tying that position to innovation and national security. It does not decide the case, establish that every dataset was lawfully acquired or stored, or resolve questions about model outputs and market substitution.



Now here is the complexity publishers seized on immediately. 

News outlets including the New York Times filed a response saying the DOJ's legal analysis that training AI models on unlicensed content is fair use contradicts that of the US Copyright Office and lacks evidentiary context.

 

The brief further accuses the federal government of ignoring completely the feasibility of licensing to accomplish the department's claimed objectives, despite the White House's previous guidance that such licensing markets should be encouraged.

 That is a pointed legal argument. The publishers are essentially saying: your own prior administration told us licensing was the preferred market solution. Now you are telling the court licensing is unnecessary. That internal government inconsistency may carry some weight with the judge.

The licensing market dynamic is the most important part of this story for you as GC. 

The Disney-OpenAI agreement shows a trend toward formal licensing of copyrighted material for generative AI use, setting the stage for how entertainment companies monetize IP in AI-driven platforms.
