[DEEP DIVE]

We are going to spend the next ten minutes on the OpenAI copyright multidistrict litigation, because this case is not abstract for Firecrown. It is specifically about whether companies built AI models on the kind of journalism, analysis, and specialty media content that FreightWaves, FLYING, Trains.com, and your other brands produce every day.

Here is where the case stands. 

MDL 3143, in the Southern District of New York, involves cross-motions for summary judgment filed September 4, 2026. Briefing runs into late November, with no ruling and no trial date set yet.

 The plaintiffs — 

including The New York Times and other news publishers, Ziff Davis, and a group of authors led by the Authors Guild

 — argue that training a large language model on their works without a license is straightforward infringement. OpenAI and Microsoft argue fair use.

The fair-use analysis in this case will run the standard four-factor test, but the battle is really being fought on factors one and four: the purpose and character of the use, and the effect on the potential market. OpenAI's position has been stated clearly. 

Public Knowledge urged the court to analyze training on its own, apart from how the data was obtained and what the models produce, and contended that training is fair use since the models learn uncopyrightable information about how language works without standing in for the source material.

 The plaintiffs push back hard on factor four. 

One argument holds that no workable market could form for licensing the volume of material needed for training.

 Publishers dispute that claim — they argue a licensing market is exactly what should exist, and that OpenAI's refusal to pay for it is the injury.

Judge Stein's willingness to accept fifty-six amicus briefs tells you two things: this court understands the case has industry-wide implications, and it wants a comprehensive record before ruling. 

As of November 2026, US copyright law requires a human author, and fair use for generative AI training is undecided on appeal.

 That is the current legal state of play. No appellate court has settled this. The SDNY ruling, when it comes, will be the first major district-court decision on the merits of the fair-use question in the LLM training context. And there is a Ninth Circuit oral argument on a related question scheduled for tomorrow, November 18th.

What does this mean for a GC at a media holding company? Three analytical frames.

First, licensing posture. If the court rules against fair use, every AI company that scraped your content without a license is a potential defendant. FreightWaves has a substantial archive of freight market reporting. FLYING has decades of aviation journalism. If that content was used in training data — and the circumstantial evidence suggests it likely was — your company may have a claim. You should be inventorying what you have and when it was published, now, before any statute of limitations analysis becomes urgent.

Second, licensing as revenue. Several publishers have already executed licensing agreements with AI companies. The New York Times sued rather than license; The Associated Press went the other direction and struck a deal. Dow Jones has pursued a similar path. 

Dow Jones aims for a billion dollars in annual earnings within five years amid its shift to digital and AI-driven products, with growth driven in part by AI licensing, risk, and energy data.

 For specialty B-to-B media with authoritative vertical expertise — which is exactly what FreightWaves is — the AI licensing calculus is worth examining. If a court establishes that training requires a license, you want to already be in a negotiating position, not scrambling to file suit.

Third, the AI products Firecrown itself may build. SONAR's Coverage Guide, launched recently as an AI-powered freight market intelligence offering, and any AI features you embed across Firecrown brands, are themselves exposed to the question of what training data underlies third-party model APIs you use. Standard contractual representations from AI vendors about clean training data are going to become a standard ask in vendor agreements across your portfolio.

Your three concrete actions. One: Commission a content audit. Know what proprietary editorial content across all Firecrown brands was indexed by major AI crawlers prior to any opt-out measures you implemented. You need a date map before you can assess any claim. Two: Get robots.txt and AI crawler blocking policies current across every property.
