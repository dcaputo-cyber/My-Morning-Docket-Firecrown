[DEEP DIVE]

Now to the deep dive: the OpenAI copyright multidistrict litigation, MDL three one four three, and what it means for a media holding company in your position.

Here is where the case stands. 

The New York Times and other publishers allege OpenAI and Microsoft copied their work to train ChatGPT. The case is at summary judgment, with cross-motions filed September fourth, twenty twenty-six, briefing running into late November, and no trial date set.

 

All sides filed summary judgment motions on September fourth. Oppositions were filed October twenty-third, and reply briefs are due November twentieth — this Thursday.



What makes this case structurally significant is that both sides have asked the judge to decide the central question without a trial. 

On November sixth, U.S. District Judge Sidney Stein in Manhattan allowed fifty-six amicus — friend of the court — briefs in the lawsuit against OpenAI and Microsoft. In September, the parties sought a ruling without a trial on whether training on copyrighted works is fair use. The ruling could change licensing economics and the legal risk profile of the entire tech industry.



The amicus posture tells you something about the stakes. 

The MDL combines copyright lawsuits from The New York Times and other news publishers, Ziff Davis, and a group of authors led by the Authors Guild. Public Knowledge, in its brief, urged the court to analyze training independently from how data was obtained and what models produce, arguing that training is fair use since models learn uncopyrightable information about language without standing in for the source material.

 On the other side, copyright scholars and publishers argue that the fourth factor — market effect — has been systematically ignored. 

One scholar's brief flagged whether copyright should recognize what he calls indirect expressive substitution: a system designed around a particular source with the goal of supplanting it.

 That framing is specifically pointed at AI systems trained on journalism that then answer questions in a way that reduces the reader's incentive to visit the original publisher.

This is not an abstract legal question for Firecrown. Here are three specific pressure points.

FreightWaves and FLYING both produce original reporting that is high-value, niche, and verifiable. If the court finds that training on copyrighted news content is fair use, your content becomes a freely available training resource for any AI company without negotiation or compensation. The AI licensing agreements that some larger publishers — including several in the MDL — negotiated as settlements or commercial deals become moot as a strategy for smaller publishers, because AI companies would have no legal obligation to license what they can take for free.

Conversely, if the court rules against OpenAI and Microsoft, the precedent could strengthen your negotiating position in any AI content partnership, make third-party scrapers more legally vulnerable, and potentially create an obligation for AI companies to license content retroactively. That is a very different economic environment for a media company that creates original, data-rich journalism.

There is a third scenario that the case structure actually enables. Because both sides asked for summary judgment — no trial — Judge Stein could issue a narrow ruling that decides the fair use question on specific facts of this record without creating the kind of clean appellate precedent that settles the law broadly. Narrowly constructed decisions are more common in novel technology cases. That means even a ruling in the near term may not definitively resolve the question for every type of content or every type of training methodology. 

As of early November, no appellate opinion has decided this question for generative AI. As of November twenty twenty-six, U.S. copyright law requires a human author, and fair use for generative AI training remains undecided on appeal.



Concurrently in the UK, 

in November twenty twenty-five, the High Court of England and Wales ruled that Stability AI was not guilty of copyright infringement despite using Getty Images as a training set for its image generator

 — but that ruling is now under appeal, with 

Getty telling the Court of Appeal last week that Stability AI did infringe by training on its protected works.

 The UK is heading in a different direction from the U.S. on the same question. For Firecrown brands with UK presence, that divergence matters.

Three concrete actions for you as GC.

One: audit your copyright notices and terms of service on every Firecrown digital property this month. If your ToS does not explicitly prohibit automated scraping and AI training ingestion, you have no contractual claim against an AI company that harvests your content — regardless of how the litigation resolves. Plain, enforceable language in your ToS is the low-cost hedge that works in every legal outcome.

Two: if FreightWaves or any other Firecrown brand receives an inbound inquiry from an AI company about data or content licensing, do not negotiate informally. Treat the first conversation as the opening of a licensing negotiation and document it. The post-ruling value of your content — in either direction — is determined by what paper trail you have now. Companies that locked in licensing agreements before the ruling have a defensible commercial framework. Companies that allowed informal use have neither.

Three: watch the November twentieth docket in MDL three one four three. Reply briefs will be public. Even if there is no immediate ruling, the briefs will tell you which arguments Judge Stein is likely to find compelling based on what each side chose to rebut. That is intelligence worth reviewing with outside copyright counsel before year-end.
