On September 30, fourteen days from today, the Information Commissioner's Office formally becomes the Information Commission. The change flows from the Data Use and Access Act 2025, and it restructures how the regulator is governed rather than what it can do. The ICO has signaled that its 2026 priority work program includes AI and biometrics supervision, a revised children's code, and the final enforcement procedural guidance that will replace the 2018 Regulatory Action Policy. Item three. The FTC issued a proposed policy statement on July 1 that deserves attention even though the public comment period has now closed. The statement addresses what the Commission calls the suppression of accuracy in artificial intelligence systems. The FTC's argument, issued pursuant to an executive order signed last December, is that AI developers who alter model outputs in ways that differ from what users reasonably expect may be engaging in deceptive conduct under Section 5 of the FTC Act. The statement specifically references situations where AI companies modify outputs to comply with state laws, and it signals that such modifications do not automatically create a safe harbor from federal enforcement if the alterations distort accuracy without clear notification. As of September 8, the statement remains in proposed status with no binding regulation attached to it. But the direction of travel is clear: the Commission under Chairman Andrew Ferguson is moving toward a framework where the question is not whether your AI product makes exaggerated claims, but whether its outputs diverge from what your users were told to expect, regardless of why that divergence occurred.

The comment period closed in April. [DEEP DIVE]

The news-publisher AI copyright litigation is not one case. It is a system of cases, and the moving parts have reached a point of sufficient legal clarity that a general counsel of a publishing company needs to hold the full picture in one frame.

Start with the state of the law on training data. In June 2025, Judge William Alsup in the Northern District of California issued the foundational ruling in the Bartz versus Anthropic case. He found that training AI models on lawfully acquired copyrighted books is fair use, but that storing pirated copies of those same works is not. That case then settled, with Anthropic paying one point five billion dollars covering four hundred eighty-two thousand four hundred sixty works at roughly three thousand one hundred nine dollars per work. Final approval came in July 2026. Three hundred fifty claimants who opted out are now pursuing separate litigation chasing the statutory maximum of one hundred fifty thousand dollars per work.

The Third Circuit heard argument on AI training fair use on June 11. Twelve weeks later, no decision. That absence matters. As of today, no US appellate court has ruled on AI training fair use. The district court consensus is that training on lawfully obtained works leans toward transformative, but the appellate courts have not confirmed it.

Meanwhile, in New York, the publishers are fighting on different terrain. The New York Times case is not primarily about training data. It is about outputs. The central theory is that ChatGPT produces text that competes with and potentially substitutes for the Times's own journalism, which damages the market for the original works. That is the fourth factor in the fair use analysis, and it is the one where the case looks strongest for the plaintiffs.

The discovery battle has been as important as any substantive ruling. In January, Judge Sidney Stein affirmed the order requiring OpenAI to produce twenty million de-identified ChatGPT output logs. OpenAI had initially agreed to twenty million logs, then tried in October 2025 to narrow the production to only conversations implicating the plaintiffs' specific works. The court rejected that approach. The logs of what ChatGPT produces across a broad range of queries, not just those involving plaintiffs' content, are relevant because they go to whether ChatGPT outputs compete with or substitute for copyrighted works. That ruling has two implications beyond this case. First, any AI company facing copyright claims should assume broad output log discovery is now the norm, not the exception. Second, the argument that user privacy protects those logs was rejected, with the court finding that users voluntarily submitted their communications and that de-identification plus a protective order adequately balances the privacy interest.

Then came July. The sanctions motion. The publishers allege that OpenAI's privacy engineering head revealed in a February deposition that the company had been able to search its training datasets and output logs throughout the discovery period, despite representing to the court that it could not do so. If the court grants sanctions, the consequences could range from adverse inference instructions to case-dispositive relief. No ruling has been issued yet. Watch for it.

In parallel, the Elsevier versus Meta case is at the pleadings stage with Meta raising copyright misuse and unclean hands as defenses. The publishers have asked to strike those defenses. That procedural skirmish matters because unclean hands has been raised by multiple AI defendants, and this is the first time a plaintiff has challenged it head-on. However the court rules, the decision will travel to other cases.

What does this mean concretely for a media holding company with multiple brands and a substantial content archive?

First action: catalog your content's commercial vulnerability. The fair use analysis hinges on market harm. The stronger the case that your journalism, your freight data insights, your aviation reporting, or your boating guides compete directly with AI-generated substitutes in the same market, the stronger your copyright claim if an AI company trained on your work. Conduct an audit of which brands have the clearest market-substitution argument and document it.

Second action: review your AI licensing posture. Several publishers, including the Times, have struck licensing deals with AI companies while simultaneously litigating. That dual-track approach is becoming the industry norm. If you have not evaluated whether licensing your content archives to AI developers generates more value than leaving that question to litigation, now is the time. Anthropic, Google, and several others have active licensing programs.

Third action: check your content's data use disclosures. If your brands license subscriber data, behavioral data, or content to any AI-adjacent third party, verify that your privacy policies and subscriber terms authorize that use. The CCPA enforcement record shows that the California AG and CalPrivacy are actively auditing vendor relationships and data flows. A content licensing deal that was not contemplated in your user-facing disclosures is a potential enforcement target, not just a litigation risk.

The data consistently identifies two areas where purpose-built legal AI produces measurable time savings quickly: contract review and regulatory monitoring. Both are high-volume, repeatable, and structured enough to benefit from AI acceleration without requiring the tool to make the judgment calls that belong to counsel. The Wolters Kluwer Future Ready Lawyer report found that sixty-two percent of legal AI users save between six and twenty percent of their workweek. If your team is not seeing time back within a month of deployment, the tool is not fitting the workflow.

The one caution worth stating plainly: courts have consistently held that when AI-generated work product causes a problem, whether a hallucinated citation or a missed deadline, the responsibility runs to counsel. The duty to supervise AI output is not delegable.
