[HEADLINE ROUNDUP]

Item one. The FTC personalized pricing comment period ends today.

On August nineteenth, the Federal Trade Commission voted two to zero to publish a proposed enforcement policy statement on personalized pricing. The Commission's position is that using a consumer's personal data to set an individualized price, without disclosing that you are doing it, may violate Section Five of the FTC Act as an unfair or deceptive act or practice. The agency is not seeking to ban the practice. Chairman Andrew Ferguson was explicit about that, saying the FTC does not have legal authority to prohibit personalized pricing in all circumstances. But the statement sets a disclosure floor: when a price is personalized, businesses must clearly and conspicuously tell the consumer that the price is personalized, why it is personalized, and what categories of data were used. The comment period, originally set to close September eighteenth, was extended by seven days. Comments in Docket FTC-2026-1057 are therefore due today. For Firecrown's e-commerce operation, Spacestore, and for any subscription product that adjusts introductory pricing by geography or behavioral signals, this is worth a hard look before year-end. Several states have already moved past disclosure toward outright prohibition on certain personalized pricing practices, so the federal baseline is genuinely a floor, not a ceiling.

Item two. CalPrivacy's enforcement machine is now running at scale.

Since California renamed its privacy agency CalPrivacy, the rebrand has not softened anything. The agency now has more than one hundred active investigations running simultaneously, and its enforcement record this year should focus any media company with California readers. In May, General Motors paid twelve point seven five million dollars in a settlement with the California Attorney General, four district attorneys, and CalPrivacy together, in what stands as the largest CCPA penalty in the law's history, covering connected-vehicle data sales and data minimization failures. Earlier this year, Disney paid two point seven five million dollars to the California AG in February, and PlayOn Sports paid one point one million dollars to CalPrivacy in March over student data and dark patterns. The Audits Division, which CalPrivacy stood up formally this year, is now conducting both announced and unannounced compliance audits. The current enforcement focus has shifted, according to the agency itself, from notice-of-violation sweeps toward systemic investigations of opt-out friction, Global Privacy Control processing, connected-vehicle data, and youth data handling. For a media holding company with subscription products, newsletter platforms, and an e-commerce store, each of those categories is a live exposure.

This is the deep dive topic, so I will keep the headline short. On September fourth, OpenAI, Microsoft, and a coalition of five news publishers including the New York Times, the Daily News, Ziff Davis, The Intercept, and the Center for Investigative Reporting each moved for summary judgment in the consolidated multidistrict litigation before Judge Sidney Stein in the Southern District of New York. The companies contest the characterization vigorously and maintain their training is transformative fair use. The Justice Department filed a Statement of Interest on September first supporting the fair-use defense at the training stage on national security and innovation grounds. None of that resolves anything yet. Judge Stein has not ruled. But the evidentiary atmosphere has shifted in a way that affects every content licensing negotiation in this industry. Item four. The UK Data Use and Access Act is fully in force, and the ICO is reorganizing itself.

The Data Use and Access Act, which received Royal Assent in June twenty twenty-five, has now fully phased in. The final requirement, that all controllers operate a formal data-protection complaints-handling procedure with a thirty-day acknowledgment obligation, came into force on June nineteenth. The PECR fine cap rose from five hundred thousand pounds to seventeen point five million pounds, aligned with UK GDPR maxima, which means a direct-marketing or cookie violation now carries the same financial ceiling as a data breach. The ICO itself is mid-transformation: the Information Commissioner, previously a corporation sole, is being reconstituted as the Information Commission, a statutory board, expected to complete during twenty twenty-six and twenty twenty-seven. For Firecrown's UK-facing operations, particularly any brands with subscribers or newsletter audiences in Britain, the combination of higher PECR fines and a newly empowered regulator with explicit guidance on enforcement discretion during the transition demands a line item in the compliance calendar.

Item five. The SEC's Regulation S-K reform is moving toward formal rulemaking.

In January, SEC Chairman Paul Atkins announced a comprehensive review of Regulation S-K, the central framework for non-financial disclosure by public companies. His stated goal is to refocus the regulation on material information and eliminate requirements that generate disclosures a reasonable investor would not consider important. The Division of Corporation Finance, under Director James Moloney, confirmed in February that the SEC is moving quickly to advance rulemaking supporting those goals. One specific proposal that has entered circulation would eliminate or restructure the quarterly earnings reporting requirement, a concept that originated in discussions at the White House and was picked up by the SEC's reform process. A formal rulemaking proposal is expected in twenty twenty-six, though new rules are unlikely to be in effect before the twenty twenty-seven reporting season. For a private media holding company like Firecrown, direct SEC reporting obligations are not the issue. The relevance is in acquisitions and exits involving public counterparties, in board governance documentation practices that sometimes mirror public-company standards, and in the ongoing question of how much voluntary disclosure serves or exposes the enterprise.

Item six. AI generative tools have crossed a threshold for in-house legal adoption, but daily use remains thin.

The numbers are striking in both directions. The FTI Consulting and Relativity General Counsel Report published in March found that eighty-seven percent of general counsel report AI use within their legal teams, up from forty-four percent just one year earlier. Summarization and clause identification are the most common applications. But the Bloomberg Law State of Practice survey published in June found that only twenty-three percent of in-house lawyers use AI tools daily. The gap between teams that have a seat and teams that have a tool they genuinely depend on is the story of the market right now. Legal-specific platforms have attracted significant capital this year. That is the GC bench segment topic, where we will bring it to ground level.

[DEEP DIVE]

The NYT versus OpenAI summary judgment, and why it is the most important pending case for a media company's IP strategy.

Let me set the table. In December twenty twenty-three, the New York Times sued OpenAI and Microsoft over the use of Times content to train large language models and the reproduction of that content in model outputs. The case survived a motion to dismiss in April twenty twenty-five. It was consolidated into a multidistrict litigation in the Southern District of New York, numbered 25-md-3143. Over the past three years, the case has been joined by book publishers, individual authors, and Ziff Davis, which brought claims covering more than forty-five digital properties.

On September fourth, all parties moved for summary judgment simultaneously. That is unusual. It means both sides believe the factual record supports a ruling in their favor without trial. OpenAI's brief counted twenty-four verbatim reproductions in twenty million chat logs reviewed. The publishers counted ten point eight million articles taken without permission or payment. The records barely overlap, and the black bars in the public versions of both briefs cover much of what each side considers its strongest evidence.

Then on September seventeenth, the Times' unredacted filing was unsealed. It contained internal communications from OpenAI and Microsoft executives. The most explosive quote is a Microsoft executive characterizing the large language model content scraping operation as "an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions." Microsoft and OpenAI dispute the characterization and have moved to contest the framing in subsequent filings. Their legal position is that training a large language model on copyrighted text is highly transformative, that the outputs do not substitute for the original journalism, and that fair use under the four-factor test protects them. The Department of Justice, filing a Statement of Interest on September first, urged Judge Stein to treat training-stage copying as fair use, citing innovation and national security interests. The DOJ did not address acquisition, storage, or output questions. It did not decide the case.

The judicial landscape beyond this case gives important context. In June twenty twenty-five, Judge William Alsup in the Northern District of California ruled in Bartz versus Anthropic that training a general-purpose AI model can be highly transformative, a factor favoring fair use. That ruling influenced the emerging judicial consensus, but it was decided on a different factual record involving different works and different systems. Thomson Reuters versus ROSS, the first AI training copyright ruling from a federal court, went the other way on the specific facts of a competing legal research product. The Copyright Office's Part Three report treats purpose, source, amount, and market effects as context-sensitive questions rather than universal outcomes.

What does the unsealed executive quote actually change? Legally, it strengthens the publishers' market-substitution argument on the fourth fair-use factor. If Microsoft's own executives believed the scraping was taking something of enormous value from publishers without compensation, that is evidence that the publishers suffered market harm, not that the training was transformative. The question of whether paywalled news archives, used to build a commercial product, create a substitute for the underlying journalism is now before the court with those internal communications in the record.

For Firecrown specifically, there are three live implications regardless of how Judge Stein rules. First, content licensing. FreightWaves, Trains.com, FLYING, and Boating and Sailing all produce original editorial content, data journalism, and data products. Any AI company seeking to train on that content now operates against a backdrop where the value of that corpus is explicitly contested and where licensing is emerging as the industry norm. The Disney-OpenAI agreement and similar arrangements represent a trend toward formal licensing as companies seek to avoid costly and uncertain litigation. A proactive licensing strategy is not just defensive. It is a revenue opportunity. Second, if the publishers prevail on any theory, the pricing of any existing AI licensing agreements will shift, and early deals signed at low rates will face pressure to reset at renewal. Third, the book-authors' case, represented by the Authors Guild, filed its own summary judgment motion on September fourth, arguing that the use of copyrighted books including material obtained from the pirated library known as LibGen was not fair use. A February court order already confirmed that an OpenAI employee downloaded books from LibGen and that those datasets were used to train GPT-3 and GPT-3.5. That factual record is separate from the news publishers' case, but a ruling on the book side may come first and will set a tone.

Three concrete actions for Firecrown's general counsel.

One: Commission a content inventory. Before the ruling, map every editorial property, data product, and archive that Firecrown holds. Identify what is already licensed to AI developers, what is scraped without agreement, and what robot-exclusion signals are in place. The DOJ's Statement of Interest distinguished acquisition and storage questions from training-stage questions, meaning companies that acquired content in legally problematic ways may not be protected even if training itself is eventually held to be fair use.

Two: Update the standard vendor AI clause. If Firecrown is currently using any AI-assisted tools, the contracts governing those tools should specify whether the vendor has the right to use your uploaded content to train or fine-tune models. The Thomson Reuters and Anthropic cases make clear that customer content submitted to AI platforms can become a downstream liability if the vendor's own training practices are later found to be infringing.

Three: Monitor the sealed executive-communications issue. The unsealed filing on September seventeenth was only possible because the court ordered disclosure of previously redacted material. There may be additional unsealing. Track the docket in 25-md-3143 and brief the executive team on the reputational and negotiating-leverage implications of any further disclosure.

[GC BENCH]

The gap between AI adoption and AI dependence, and what it means for a solo or small in-house legal function.

Two data points from this year need to sit next to each other. The ACC Chief Legal Officers Survey, covering more than one thousand CLOs across forty-three countries, found that sixty-three percent expect to keep team headcount flat, meaning AI tools are supposed to add capacity, not replace people who were going to be hired anyway. At the same time, the Bloomberg Law State of Practice survey of seven hundred and sixty practitioners found that only twenty-three percent of in-house lawyers use AI tools daily. The phrase to hold onto is: plenty of teams have a seat; far fewer have a tool they depend on.

The practical implication for a GC running a lean function with broad subject-matter coverage across privacy, IP, employment, contracts, and commercial is that the relevant question is no longer whether to adopt AI, it is which category of tool solves which specific bottleneck. The market has segmented clearly. Contract lifecycle management and matter intake tools, platforms like Ironclad and Streamline AI, solve one layer. Legal-specific AI platforms purpose-built for in-house generalists, which handle research with primary-law citations, contract drafting, regulatory memo generation, and outside-counsel matter management, solve a different layer. And horizontal platforms, ChatGPT Business, Microsoft Copilot, Claude for Work, offer flexibility but require enterprise data controls before any client content touches them.

Microsoft's April thirtieth launch of a Word Legal Agent is worth noting here specifically. It runs playbook-based clause-by-clause review with tracked-change redlines inside Word. That is a low-friction entry point for a small legal team that already lives in the Microsoft ecosystem. The open governance question for any GC deploying these tools is data residency and retention. Where does the content uploaded for review sit, and does the vendor's model benefit from it? That question belongs in the contract evaluation, not the post-deployment audit.

One metric worth tracking as a benchmark: Thomson Reuters CoCounsel estimates in-house counsel can save up to two hundred and forty hours annually using professional-grade AI tools. That figure is vendor-reported and should be interrogated, but it gives a floor for the business case when justifying tooling spend to a CFO.

[INDUSTRY ANGLE]

Freight, air cargo, and the Q4 setup.

Start with trucking, because FreightWaves and SONAR produced the most direct signal of the week. At the SONAR and Cass Shippers Forum held at JFK on September fifteenth, Craig Fuller and Zach Strickland addressed a room of shippers and used the word fragile. That framing is significant precisely because it does not mean weak. The SONAR Tender Rejection Index climbed above seventeen percent in June, the highest reading since twenty twenty-two, then pulled back below fourteen percent before jumping roughly seventy-five basis points in a single week heading into the event. Strickland told the audience that the volatility itself is the problem. A market that cannot hold its equilibrium for more than a few weeks is one where small demand moves can force large price adjustments. Fuller's read is that the fragility traces to capacity tightness, not demand collapse. Two years of carrier exits contracted the supply side. The market is now reactive in ways that a healthier, more saturated capacity environment would absorb without notice.

The Cass Truckload Linehaul Index reached a hundred and fifty-two point nine in July, up eight point six percent year over year. ACT Research projects that index to continue higher through Q4 as new contract executions from the July bid cycle flow through and spot market tightening reinforces the direction. National on-highway diesel averaged five dollars and fifty-nine cents per gallon at the end of August, with California at seven dollars and twenty-two cents. The EIA projects that September and October refinery maintenance will tighten refined product supply further, adding cost pressure heading into the peak season.

For the aviation side, data from the TAC Index published on September twenty-first showed the global Baltic Air Freight Index up twenty point nine percent versus a year ago, with the most recent weekly reading a gain of roughly one percent. Global air cargo demand grew six percent year over year in August, according to Xeneta. Rates remain firm entering peak season, and the structural driver is capacity that has not recovered. Earlier this year, Middle East airspace disruptions forced airlines onto longer northern and southern corridors, extending flight times and fuel burn. IATA's June outlook documented the pressure on unit costs.

The net freight picture as Firecrown enters its fourth quarter is this: the platforms that serve trucking, aviation, and rail audiences are reporting into a market where operational costs are elevated, capacity is tight but fragile, and the data intelligence layer, precisely the kind of data SONAR provides, has genuine commercial and policy value. The U.S. Department of Transportation formalized that when it signed on as a SONAR customer earlier this year to monitor the safety, security, and economic viability of the freight market in near real time.
