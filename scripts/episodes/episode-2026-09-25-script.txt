First item. The FTC moved yesterday — literally yesterday, September 24th — on a topic that every ad-supported publisher needs to understand immediately. 

The Commission announced it is considering whether to update its Rule on Impersonation of Government and Businesses, or take other action to prevent online platforms from engaging in ad-optimization practices that may be furthering impersonation scams.

 

In an Advance Notice of Proposed Rulemaking, the FTC is seeking comment on the extent to which ad-optimization tools and services offered by social media, search engines, and other digital marketplace platforms further efforts by scammers to impersonate legitimate businesses and government agencies using online ads.

 

The ANPRM seeks public comment on potential new obligations for social media, search engines, and digital marketplace platforms to prevent fraudulent ads from reaching consumers.

 For a media holding company that relies on programmatic advertising revenue, this matters doubly. If rulemaking progresses, platforms will face new compliance costs that will almost certainly be passed downstream. And the FTC's willingness to expand its impersonation rule framework is a signal that the commission is prepared to treat ad-optimization as a regulated activity, not merely a business practice.

Second item. Also from the FTC, and today marks its public comment deadline — the Commission's proposed enforcement policy statement on personalized pricing closes at midnight tonight. 

The FTC announced it is seeking public comment on an enforcement policy statement regarding personalized pricing, which is the use of personal data to set prices according to the amount that a company believes an individual consumer is willing to spend. FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson said, "When consumers see a listed price, they expect it to be the same price that everyone else sees, not the retailer's estimate of how much they are willing to pay based on their personal data."

 

The proposal says businesses may violate Section 5 of the FTC Act if they do not clearly explain when a price is personalized, why it is personalized, and what data is used.

 For a publisher running dynamic subscription pricing — testing introductory rates, stepping up renewals, varying prices by device or user history — this proposal is highly relevant. The federal baseline, as written, is disclosure rather than prohibition. But several states have already gone further. New Jersey, for instance, enacted the Fair Price Protection Act, which outright prohibits using personal data to vary prices for certain consumer goods. Watch how this final policy statement reads when it comes out.

Third item. The UK's data protection landscape changes structurally in five days. 

On September 10th, the government made the Data Use and Access Act 2025 Commencement Number 9 Regulations 2026, which appoints September 30th, 2026 as the day on which the provisions that replace the office of Information Commissioner with the Information Commission come into force. Those three provisions carry out the governance reform that Parliament wrote into the Data Use and Access Act 2025: replacing the office of Information Commissioner, a corporation sole held by one person, with the Information Commission, a body corporate with collective decision-making.

 

This milestone follows the announcement in July of the seven Non-Executive Members who have been appointed to the new Information Commission Board, who will assume their new roles on September 30th. The organization will continue to be known as the ICO.

 For Firecrown's UK-facing brands, the practical question is continuity. 

The commencement regulations' saving provision treats anything in progress with the Information Commissioner immediately before September 30th as continuing, undisturbed, with the Information Commission afterwards.

 But the regulatory posture will evolve. A board makes different decisions than a sole commissioner. Watch the first public statements from the new board carefully.

Fourth item. California's privacy enforcement machine continues to run at full speed.

 The agency's enforcement approach is now producing a second wave of case law after the Tractor Supply and Ford settlements earlier this year. 

In March 2026, CalPrivacy announced a three hundred seventy-five thousand dollar decision against Ford Motor Company, finding that the automaker created unnecessary friction by improperly processing consumer requests to opt out of the sale or sharing of personal information. Ford used a standardized form for all CCPA requests, including the right to opt out, and then required consumers to respond to a follow-up email to verify their identity. The CCPA does not provide a similar verification process for opting out of data selling or sharing.

 The takeaway for any media company using standardized privacy request workflows: opt-out verification is legally distinct from deletion verification. Do not treat them the same way.

[DEEP DIVE]

The Manhattan summary judgment filings in the OpenAI copyright litigation deserve a full unpacking, because the decision Judge Stein eventually renders — expected in coming months on whether to send the case to trial — will set the operating framework for how any publisher licenses, or fails to license, its archive.

Let us start with what was actually filed. 

OpenAI counted twenty-four verbatim reproductions in twenty million chat logs. The publishers counted ten point eight million articles taken.

 Those two numbers tell you everything about why this is so hard. The parties are not merely arguing about the law. They are arguing about what the evidence shows, and they are describing the same technical system in incompatible factual terms.

The DOJ's intervention shifted the political landscape of the case without shifting the legal one. 

The filing argues that training large language models on copyrighted text generally qualifies as fair use and warns that restricting this practice would harm scientific progress, economic competitiveness, and national security. The Justice Department's roughly twenty-page filing frames training LLMs as extraordinarily transformative. The brief cautions that requiring licenses for training data would impede scientific advancement, weaken U.S. economic competitiveness, and raise national-security concerns.



However, there is a critical legal distinction that the DOJ filing itself draws. 

Document 316 advocates fair use for training-stage copying, while acquisition, storage, and output questions remain separate, and the court has not adopted the position. The United States has taken a position on one part of the dispute: it argues that copying written works to train an AI model can be fair use. The filing is advocacy, not a ruling, and it separates training from acquisition, storage, and output questions.



That distinction matters enormously for a media company in Firecrown's position. The DOJ is arguing about the training stage. The publishers' strongest arguments are about outputs that substitute for the original work — about what comes out of the model, not just what went in. 

A ruling in favor of OpenAI and Microsoft would serve as a massive blow to the publishing industry, potentially undermining its ability to control and monetize how content is scraped and used by AI giants. In its motion for summary judgment, the Times argued OpenAI and Microsoft copied its works at scale to build commercial substitutes, which it believes violates the law.



And then there is the parallel development that has not received enough attention: Microsoft and OpenAI also filed separate summary judgment motions on September 4th. 

Microsoft and OpenAI told a New York federal judge that they should be absolved of all copyright claims brought by book and news publishers for allegedly training their AI models on protected works. The companies' use of copyrighted materials to develop large language models constitutes fair use, and their AI model outputs transform the ingested information enough to shield them from liability, they told the court in separate motions for summary judgment.



The publishers' response brief, filed September 17th, runs ninety-two pages. 

News publishers filed a ninety-two-page brief asking a judge to reject OpenAI and Microsoft's fair use defense in the New York copyright case.

 The publishers are leaning on internal executive communications as evidence of commercial intent — that OpenAI and Microsoft understood they were building competitive substitutes for the journalism they were ingesting.

What should Firecrown's general counsel take from this? Three concrete actions.

First, audit your current AI licensing posture. 

As of early September 2026, no single US decision makes all generative AI training lawful or unlawful.

 That means your content archives sit in a legal grey zone. Document every licensing agreement you have with AI vendors. If you have none, that is a negotiating position, not just an oversight.

Second, separate your evidence trail. 

For a publisher, preserve separate evidence about source acquisition, permissions, licensing, retention, and observed outputs.

 The litigation is turning on how courts weigh the four fair use factors against specific factual records. Your records need to be ready to support the argument that your content has independent market value that AI-generated outputs substitute, not complement.

Third, watch the Google AI Overviews litigation in parallel. 

AI Overviews have been correlated with a fifty-eight percent reduction in click-through rates to the websites whose content those summaries are built on.

 

A third of publishers surveyed say they will block AI Overviews once the tools to do so become available.

[INDUSTRY ANGLE]

The freight market from FreightWaves and SONAR this week deserves careful reading, because the word being used by Craig Fuller and Zach Strickland is not cyclical and it is not recovering — it is fragile.



The monthly FreightWaves State of Freight webinar has migrated to FreightWaves Today, but a gathering of shippers in New York brought together by SONAR got a chance to hear Craig Fuller and Zach Strickland opine on a market they described as fragile. Unlike earlier State of Freight webinars in the first part of 2026, Fuller and Strickland this time were measuring a market that was not moving higher in leaps and bounds.





The SONAR Tender Rejection Index climbed above seventeen percent in June, dropped below fourteen percent in the weeks since, then jumped roughly seventy-five basis points in a single week — a swing Strickland said undercuts any read of a settled market. "This market is so fragile, even though we're having these periods of stability," Strickland said. "That tells me that this market is not in a stable position."



Fuller offered important context. 

He cautioned against reading fragile as automatically bad. The volatility traces to capacity tightness rather than a demand collapse — a different kind of fragility than the pandemic-era chaos shippers remember.



And the cost pressure is real. 

Diesel prices surged to six dollars and twenty-six cents per gallon to start the week of September 16th, a thirty-three cent jump in a single week, according to FreightWaves' Diesel Transaction Spot index — a figure that updates daily and has already climbed above the Department of Energy's latest weekly read. The spike puts diesel fifteen percent above last month and sixty-eight percent higher than the same point last year, compressing carrier margins even as freight volumes begin to recover.



On the ocean side:

US East Coast rates remain firmer than US West Coast rates due to reduced capacity and ongoing Panama Canal draft and weight restrictions.



In aviation, the fuel cost story is the defining variable. 

Jet fuel has become the single most powerful variable in airline economics again: the global average price hit one hundred seventy-one dollars per barrel in the first week of September, up nine percent in one week and roughly fifty-seven percent above year-ago levels, forcing carriers like Ryanair to trim growth plans purely to shrink unhedged winter fuel exposure.

 

Higher fuel costs are compressing earnings while rising fares increase passenger costs, creating pressure on demand and customer sentiment. June traffic totaled eighty-seven and a half million passengers, down from the June 2024 record, signaling softer volume at a time when airlines are relying on pricing to sustain revenue.
