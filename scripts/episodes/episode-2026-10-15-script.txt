[DEEP DIVE]

Today's deep dive: California's expanded deletion rights under Senate Bill 923 and what they require from a media holding company with multiple digital properties, reader e-commerce, and layered subscriber data.

Start with the scope shift. Under the prior CCPA framework, 

California consumers could request deletion of their personal information, but that right covered only information collected directly from the consumer.

 

Senate Bill 923 — the Expanding Privacy Rights Act — amends the CCPA to require businesses to delete personal information collected from or about a consumer, rather than only information collected directly from that consumer.

 That three-letter preposition swap — from to about — is not cosmetic. It captures data your business obtained from a data broker, a co-registration partner, a list purchase, an audience enrichment service, or inferred from behavioral signals. If your brand enriches subscriber profiles with third-party demographic overlays, every record in that overlay is now potentially subject to a deletion request from a California resident.

Layer in the enforcement picture. 

Early 2026 saw three significant enforcement actions under CCPA, with combined penalties exceeding four point two million dollars. Together, they reveal intensifying regulatory focus on how businesses implement opt-out mechanisms, comply with opt-out preference signals, and facilitate consumer rights requests.

 The cases that matter most for this room are the ones involving media and sports-adjacent digital platforms. 

In Q1 2026 alone, California regulators issued over four million dollars in fines across three enforcement actions — Disney at two point seven five million, PlayOn Sports at one point one million, and Ford at three hundred and seventy-five thousand.

 Disney is directly analogous to a multi-brand digital publisher. They failed to honor opt-outs account-wide across linked services. 

The compliance order requires Disney to implement opt-out choices account-wide on each web property, application, and device used to access Disney services.

 If you run FreightWaves, FLYING, Trains.com, Boating and Sailing, and Spacestore as separate front ends that share a common identity graph or subscriber database, you have a version of the same architecture that got Disney fined.

What does a media holding company specifically need to review after Senate Bill 923?

First, your data inventory. 

CalPrivacy's enforcement orders now require businesses to maintain comprehensive inventories of tracking tools used on their websites and to conduct internal audits and monitoring to ensure that opt-out choices are fully applied to those technologies.

 Do you know every third-party audience segment, every lookalike model, every behavioral dataset that lives in your advertising or editorial stack? If your answer is partial, that is your starting point.

Second, your deletion workflows. A deletion request from a California subscriber now triggers an obligation that runs to data you did not collect directly. That means you need to map your data supply chain upstream. Which data vendors provided enriched records about your subscribers? Do your vendor contracts include data deletion pass-through provisions? Most do not unless you specifically negotiated them, and Senate Bill 923 creates the pressure to address that in your next contract cycle.

Third, 

online-only businesses with direct relationships with consumers previously only had to offer an email address for privacy rights requests. Now they must also offer an online method, such as a web form or an online portal.

 If any Firecrown property is currently honoring requests only by email, that needs to change now.

Fourth, the cookie and analytics intersection. Under the parallel changes from the Data Use and Access Act in the UK, there is a divergence from the US framework worth noting. In the UK, 

the analytics exemption is particularly significant. If your sole purpose is collecting aggregate statistics to improve your website, you can now run analytics without consent — but you must clearly explain the use, offer a simple free way to opt out, and ensure the data cannot be used to identify individual visitors. The ICO has been explicit that advertising-related activities sit outside those exemptions.

 So the UK is loosening on aggregate analytics while remaining strict on advertising. California is tightening on deletion scope while also loosening some CIPA exposure via SB 690. You have a patchwork, and you need jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction mapping of your properties.

Three concrete actions for in-house GC.

One: conduct a deletion request audit. Pull the last ninety days of deletion requests across all Firecrown properties and trace each one through to completion. Identify any request that was fulfilled only as to directly collected data and flag it for remediation under the expanded standard.

Two: add deletion pass-through clauses to your data vendor agreements. The next renewal cycle for any third-party data provider — any enrichment vendor, any co-registration partner, any audience extension platform — should include an explicit obligation on the vendor's part to honor deletion requests that you pass through. Set a deadline for every existing vendor relationship.

Three: commission a cross-property data flow diagram before year-end. Map how subscriber identity, behavioral data, and third-party enrichment data move between FreightWaves, FLYING, Trains.com, Boating and Sailing, and Spacestore. That diagram is your compliance roadmap and your litigation defense. You want it documented, dated, and updated annually.
