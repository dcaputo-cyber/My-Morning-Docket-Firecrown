First item. 

The FTC and twenty states sued Dollar General on Sunday, October 26, for what regulators described as long-running deceptive pricing practices, alleging that Dollar General routinely charges customers more at the register than advertised prices indicate.

 That case is straightforward consumer protection, but it sits inside a broader FTC pattern worth tracking. 

The Publicis-LiveRamp consent order, issued October 19, is the sixth major FTC enforcement action against the digital advertising ecosystem in 2026 alone.

 

Under the proposed order, Publicis and LiveRamp must, for five years, provide non-discriminatory access on fair terms to LiveRamp's standalone products and services, and are explicitly prohibited from denying, degrading, delaying, or impairing any customer's access.

 

The FTC opted for behavioral remedies rather than structural separation, a choice consistent with the trajectory courts and regulators have taken in digital-markets antitrust cases throughout 2025 and 2026, including a September 2026 ruling in which a federal judge rejected Google ad-tech divestiture in favor of behavioral obligations.

 The message for media companies that depend on LiveRamp for identity resolution is nuanced. The platform stays intact, but the order creates a public comment period through November 19th. 

The public has thirty days from October 19 to submit comments via Regulations.gov.

 If you use LiveRamp, that comment window is worth your attention.

Second item. California's fall privacy law package. 

California enacted several significant privacy law changes in 2026, including expanded CCPA deletion rights, limits on certain California Invasion of Privacy Act claims for certain online activities, shorter data broker deletion timelines, and new insurance privacy requirements. Notably, SB 923 expands deletion rights under the CCPA to cover third-party-sourced data.

 That last piece is the one that matters most for publishers. Until now, deletion rights only covered data collected directly from the consumer. Now they reach data a publisher received from a third-party data provider, a list vendor, or an ad-tech partner. 

As of October 12, 2026, the CCPA enforcement tracker shows twelve actions taken in 2026 with combined penalties exceeding eighteen point one million dollars.

 The enforcement infrastructure is clearly operational. Review your third-party data intake and your deletion workflows before the end of Q4.

[DEEP DIVE]

Now the deep dive. The Meta scraping terms crisis, and what it means for a publisher operating business accounts on Meta's platforms, which is to say, nearly every publisher in the country.

Let's reconstruct the timeline. 

On October 16, reports surfaced flagging broad scraping language in Meta's consolidated commercial terms. On October 17, Meta removed the word "train" from the disputed clause and issued a public statement.

 Then the News/Media Alliance weighed in. 

The largest news publishing trade association in the United States called on Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg to suspend the implementation of the company's new commercial terms of service, set to go into effect on October 30. In an open letter, NMA president and CEO Danielle Coffey wrote that Meta should withdraw these terms and hold discussions with publishers.



What do the current revised terms actually say? 

The revised terms state that businesses which continue to use Meta's platforms after October 30 authorize Meta to use automated software, including web scrapers, bots, robots, spiders, crawlers, and user agents, to access, index, cache, analyze, collect, retrieve, or crawl the domains, web pages, or URLs associated with their products or services.



Meta's public position is that the crawling is limited to advertising and business products, and that robots.txt preferences are respected. The legal community is not satisfied with that assurance. 

Meta has not included any specific language about respecting robots.txt preferences in its revised terms of service. The new terms are slated to go into effect on October 30. There is no option for business accounts to opt out before then.





According to James Grimmelmann, a professor of digital and information law at Cornell Law School: "For news publishers, this clearly represents permission to Meta to train AI on any content from their sites and possibly to deploy some of that content, even in competition with the publishers themselves. On the broadest possible interpretation, this would be used to develop a comprehensive archive of all content that Meta would then serve as it wanted, directly or through AI."



The binary that publishers face as of today is stark. 

There is currently no option for business accounts to opt out of these new terms ahead of October 30. The update reads: if you do not agree to the updated terms, please stop all access or use of our products for business or commercial purposes.





"This is a remarkable provision to bury in terms of service, given that this would give Meta access to material that would likely cost many millions of dollars in a licensing market," said Matt Topic, a media and intellectual property attorney at Loevy and Loevy.



Now, European publishers have gone further. 

European publishers and advertising associations are calling for EU intervention because Meta's new terms of service would allow the company to automatically scrape websites belonging to businesses with Facebook or Instagram accounts and use that content for AI purposes without giving affected parties the opportunity to object.



What should a GC at a media holding company do right now? Three concrete actions.

One. Conduct an immediate robots.txt audit across all Firecrown properties, FreightWaves, Trains.com, FLYING, Boating and Sailing, and Spacestore. Confirm that the meta-crawlers exclusion is explicitly set. Meta says it respects these flags. Lock them in tonight. This is the fastest, most cost-free protection available and you can do it before noon.

Two. Assess your Meta business account footprint. Map every brand that has a Facebook page, Instagram profile, or WhatsApp channel. Determine whether any of those accounts can be deactivated without material commercial harm. If a property generates no measurable revenue from Meta-referred traffic, shutting the business account is a clean opt-out. Make that decision brand by brand, not portfolio-wide.

Three. Preserve your rights and build a paper record. Send a written communication to Meta via your business account management channel formally stating that Firecrown does not consent to the use of its content for AI training or for the development of products competitive with its publishing brands, and that it reserves all rights under applicable copyright law and licensing frameworks. This will not prevent crawling, but it establishes intent on the record. It also positions you favorably if this dispute enters litigation, which the News/Media Alliance's language suggests they are at least contemplating.

The deeper issue is that this is not solely a Meta problem. As the OpenAI, Google Gemini, and Anthropic cases move toward summary judgment, the news publishing industry is discovering that its content was the substrate for a multi-trillion-dollar asset class and received no compensation. The meta-terms crisis is the latest, most visible flashpoint in that same dynamic. It will not be the last.

[INDUSTRY ANGLE]

The industry angle. Freight, rail, aviation, and marine.



The SONAR Truckload Rejection Index hit an unexpected fourteen percent in October, a significant development outside normal seasonal patterns. This is not being driven by a demand spike, but rather by persistent market imbalance and insufficient supply across the country.

 

The SONAR Tender Rejection Index climbed to fourteen point one seven percent, which SONAR's Zach Strickland called a significant and unexpected development. What makes the move notable is what is not behind it: the SONAR Tender Volume Index shows no demand-side surge, meaning the tightening is being driven entirely by a supply shortfall. Strickland noted that mid-October is typically a brief breathing period before the holiday rush, making the breadth of the current disruption all the more striking.



The reefer segment is running even hotter. 

Refrigerated truckload tender rejections are running at twenty-one point four four percent nationally as of October 20, meaning roughly one in five reefer loads is being turned down by carriers, according to FreightWaves SONAR data. The SONAR Truckload Rejection Index has hovered between thirteen and fourteen percent since August.

 

SONAR's tender rejection maps show the tightest conditions concentrated in the Pacific Northwest and across parts of the Midwest, with fall harvest commodities, apples, potatoes, and onions moving out of the Pacific Northwest, and poultry and protein products shipping ahead of Thanksgiving demand.



The structural story underneath these numbers is the one Craig Fuller and SONAR's team have been tracking all year. 

As of October 2026, new trucking capacity is not entering the freight market, even after a full year of elevated tender rejections. SONAR data shows tractors in new for-hire fleets fell to nineteen thousand five hundred twenty in August, while the SONAR Truckload Rejection Index held near fourteen percent, about three times its 2023-to-2025 range.

 

Over the three years ending October 2026, the SONAR Truckload Volume Index is up nine percent, while the rejection index is up two hundred sixty-eight percent.

 New capacity is simply not responding to the price signal, which SONAR attributes partly to persistently high insurance and compliance costs making entry uneconomic for small carriers.

On the rails, 

a thirty-four percent cost discount between intermodal contract rates and truckload contract rates is pushing shippers toward rail, according to SONAR data. Truckload contract rates have risen seven and a half percent over the past three months while intermodal contract rates have remained essentially flat, up just zero point six percent over the same period.



In air cargo, 

air cargo tonnages out of Asia quickly rebounded following the Golden Week holiday, while volumes from other origins were under pressure. Boeing was forced to pause production of its 777 and 767 freighters due to a dispute with a parts supplier.

 That Boeing production halt is worth monitoring for the FLYING audience. 

Air cargo executives called for a new midsize freighter at this year's Cargo Facts Symposium in Nashville, with concern focused on what aircraft will replace the aging Boeing 767 freighters that now compose a hefty chunk of the midsize cargo fleet.



On the marine side, 

one of the strongest signals in the October freight market is the continued deterioration in container schedule reliability. Global schedule reliability fell by five point nine percentage points in August to forty-nine point nine percent, its lowest level since September 2022.

 

The Panama Canal is increasing Neopanamax transits from nine to ten vessels per day beginning October 15 and restoring the maximum authorized draft to forty-nine feet as water levels recover. At the same time, select carriers are returning to the Suez Canal, reducing India-to-US East Coast transit times by up to two weeks.
