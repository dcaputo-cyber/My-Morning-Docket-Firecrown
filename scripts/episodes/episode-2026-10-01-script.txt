[DEEP DIVE]

Let's spend some real time on the Third Circuit ruling, because it is the most consequential intellectual property development in the AI era so far, and its logic runs directly into Firecrown's editorial and commercial interests.

The case is Thomson Reuters versus Ross Intelligence. 

Thomson Reuters filed suit in twenty twenty, alleging that Ross Intelligence, which competed in the legal research market, copied material from Thomson Reuters' Westlaw platform to train Ross Intelligence's competing AI-based legal search engine.

 

On September twenty-ninth, the Philadelphia-based Third Circuit upheld Thomson Reuters' victory, rejecting Ross's argument that its use of Westlaw material to build an AI search engine counted as fair use.



The doctrinal heart of the decision rests on two pillars. First, transformativeness. The court found that Ross's use was not sufficiently transformative — it took Westlaw's editorial judgments and converted them into training data for a product that competed directly in the same market. Second, market substitution. Because Ross's product was designed to replace Westlaw in the legal research market, the use harmed the market for the original work. 

Because Ross's use of the headnotes was insufficiently transformative and aided in the startup's market competition with Westlaw, fair use did not apply.



Now let's hold that logic up against the publishing industry's current situation. 

In July twenty twenty-six, a federal court granted final approval of the one point five billion dollar settlement in Bartz versus Anthropic.

 That case had a very different fact pattern — training on lawfully acquired books, not material taken from a competitor to directly replicate that competitor's product. 

On September sixteenth, the Ninth Circuit went in the other direction on a related issue: it affirmed dismissal of a DMCA claim in Doe versus GitHub, holding that Copilot does not remove or alter copyright management information from an existing work when it generates code, because it creates a new work rather than reproducing a stored one.



So you now have three significant AI copyright data points. Bartz: training on lawfully acquired copyrighted books can be fair use, but retaining pirated copies is not. 

Yes, training an AI model on lawfully acquired copyrighted books can qualify as fair use, but retaining pirated copies, or stripping copyright management information from the data, is a separate legal violation that fair use does not excuse.

 Doe versus GitHub: generating output that is a new work does not violate the DMCA's copyright management information provisions. And now Thomson Reuters: training data taken from a competitor to build a directly competing product is copyright infringement, not fair use.

For Firecrown, the implications run in two directions. You are both a content licensor and a potential content licensee.

On the licensor side, FreightWaves, FLYING, Trains.com, and Boating and Sailing produce original editorial content — proprietary data analysis, market commentary, reviews, and journalism. That content is exactly the kind of differentiated, expert-curated material that AI companies are harvesting for training data. The Third Circuit ruling strengthens your hand significantly when an AI company scrapes your content and builds a competing freight intelligence or aviation information product. The market substitution analysis is your friend: if an AI company uses FreightWaves freight data commentary to train a product that competes with FreightWaves' own intelligence products, the court's reasoning maps directly. The question is how well you have documented the scope and originality of your editorial output, and whether your terms of service and robots.txt files make the prohibition on scraping explicit and enforceable.

On the licensee side, if Firecrown or any affiliated entity is using AI tools trained on third-party content, the Ross ruling is a caution. The key question is not whether you used a commercial AI product — it is whether the AI company that trained the model used your competitors' content to build something you then deploy in competition with them. That chain of potential liability now has appellate weight behind it.



Meanwhile, the United States government filed a Statement of Interest in the New York Times versus OpenAI case, asking the court to reject the argument that training large language models on copyrighted texts is categorically infringing, citing AI competitiveness and national security.

 The DOJ's position is that training-phase copying can be fair use, even if storage and output questions remain open. So you have the executive branch and the Third Circuit pointing in different directions. That tension will produce more litigation, more appeals, and eventually a Supreme Court case. Plan for years of uncertainty.

Three concrete actions for Firecrown's legal team.

One: audit your terms of service across all brands — FreightWaves, FLYING, Trains.com, Boating and Sailing, Spacestore — and ensure they contain explicit prohibitions on scraping for AI training purposes, with language that survives both contract and copyright analysis. Cross-check that your robots.txt configurations are consistent with those terms.

Two: if you do not have a content licensing framework for AI companies already, build one now. The Third Circuit ruling makes your content more valuable to AI developers who want to license rather than litigate. A clean licensing structure lets you monetize that interest and creates a paper trail that distinguishes authorized use from infringement.

Three: review any AI tools your teams use to generate or summarize content, and confirm with outside counsel that the training data provenance of those tools does not create derivative liability. The Ross decision, combined with the Google Gemini publisher class action filed in July, 

by a coalition including Hachette, Cengage, Elsevier, and novelist Scott Turow, alleging that Google used their copyrighted works to train Gemini models without permission and intentionally removed copyright management information embedded in the works,

 means the IP risk surface for media companies is expanding in both directions.

Freight, aviation, and ocean shipping, because those markets are the editorial and business heartbeat of several Firecrown brands.

Starting with trucking, where Craig Fuller and Zach Strickland are using one word repeatedly: fragile. 

At a SONAR and Cass Shippers Forum at JFK Airport on September fifteenth, Fuller and Strickland opined on a market they described as fragile.

 

Unlike earlier State of Freight sessions in the first part of twenty twenty-six, they were measuring a market that was not moving higher in leaps and bounds.

 

Strickland said: "This market is so fragile, even though we're having these periods of stability."



The SONAR data tells the story numerically. 

The national Tender Rejection Index climbed to fourteen percent and is holding near a secondary peak that rivals levels seen around Labor Day, with van freight driving the bulk of the pressure.

 

Van moves account for sixty to seventy percent of all freight tendered in the United States, making even modest rejection-rate increases far more consequential than comparable moves in refrigerated or flatbed.

 

The SONAR National Truckload Index seven-day average dipped two cents per mile week over week to three dollars and forty-two cents as of September sixteenth, but remains three percent above last month and forty-seven percent above last year's level.



On the ocean side, the picture is tight and getting tighter as Q4 approaches. 

As of September eleventh, the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index had risen for seven consecutive weeks to three thousand six hundred and sixty-two, just shy of its post-pandemic peak.

 

Weather-driven congestion at major Chinese ports, low water levels affecting both the Panama Canal and the Rhine and Danube rivers, and the run-up to China's Golden Week holidays are all pushing rates higher across the transpacific and into Oceania.

 

Shanghai to Los Angeles rates have climbed to seven thousand one hundred and eighty-five dollars per FEU, while approximately twenty percent of capacity has been pulled through blank sailings.



Air cargo feels the same China Golden Week pressure. 

Quarter-end shipments, China's pre-holiday export rush, and technology-related cargo could reduce departure options at some gateways.

 

Air freight demand remains up seven percent year over year, with capacity constraints starting to ease slightly compared to previous months.



For the FLYING and Boating and Sailing editorial teams and for the Spacestore e-commerce business: expect elevated inbound freight costs through at least late October, with particular pressure on transpacific lanes. This is the moment to revisit vendor agreements for any imported inventory and to evaluate whether spot market exposure on air freight for time-sensitive cargo is appropriately hedged.
