[DEEP DIVE]

Today's deep dive is the Third Circuit's ruling in Thomson Reuters versus ROSS Intelligence, which was handed down on September twenty-ninth and whose full unsealed reasoning has been analyzed extensively in the weeks since. This is the first federal appellate decision to directly address fair use in the AI training context, and its implications for a media company that both creates and licenses content are worth unpacking carefully.



The Third Circuit issued the first federal appellate decision directly addressing copyright fair use in the AI-training context. In a long-running and closely watched legal battle, the court affirmed a district court's decision that ROSS infringed the copyrights in Thomson Reuters's Westlaw headnotes by using them to train an AI-driven legal research tool.



Here is what the court actually decided and what it deliberately left open. 

The Third Circuit rejected ROSS's arguments that Westlaw's headnotes lacked sufficient originality for copyright protection and that ROSS's copying was fair use.

 Walking through the four-factor analysis: on the first factor, transformativeness, 

the Third Circuit found that ROSS's use was not sufficiently transformative for fair use purposes.

 On the fourth factor, market harm, 

Thomson Reuters already used headnotes to train its own AI search products, and the court concluded that ROSS Intelligence's unlicensed use deprived Thomson Reuters of the opportunity to license the headnotes for the same purpose.

 

The court noted that the market for licensing headnotes as text to train AI is rapidly developing.



Now here is the critical limitation. 

The Third Circuit explicitly distinguished ROSS Intelligence's AI product from systems that can generate original expression, leaving the central generative-AI debate unresolved.

 

Although the court did not rule on large language model training per se, the footnote suggests that generative AI developers may fare better on the first factor — but would still face the fourth factor, in which the Third Circuit counted the licensing market against ROSS.



So what does this mean for Firecrown? Three dimensions.

First, your content assets. FreightWaves, Trains.com, FLYING, Boating and Sailing — all of these publish editorial content that carries copyright. The ROSS ruling confirms that if an AI company used that content without a license to train a product that competes in your market, that is not automatically fair use. The court's fourth-factor reasoning — that there is a rapidly developing market for licensing content for AI training — is exactly the argument your team should be making if you are negotiating or litigating around training data use. The existence of an emerging licensing market is now a live factor in the analysis.

Second, your own AI use. If Firecrown or any of its brands is building AI-assisted tools — editorial summarization, ad copy generation, search product features — the licensing provenance of your training data matters. The ROSS court's reasoning on the third factor, the amount and substantiality of the copying, is directly relevant. 

The practical guidance emerging from this litigation is to evaluate acquisition, training, grounding, and output as distinct uses rather than treating all AI-related copying as a single activity.

 That is a framework your legal and product teams should adopt internally.

Third, the MDL in New York. 

The OpenAI and Microsoft MDL combines copyright lawsuits filed by the New York Times, Ziff Davis, the Authors Guild, and others.

 Ziff Davis is a direct peer. The outcome of that summary judgment will establish whether generative AI training on journalism falls on the same side of the line as ROSS's use of Westlaw headnotes. 

The Third Circuit's analysis found harm to both the existing market for legal research and the potential market for licensing training data — but the court left unresolved whether AI-generated works that compete with but don't copy original content can constitute cognizable harm.

 That unresolved question is the center of the generative AI dispute.

Three concrete actions for your team. One: audit the licensing status of any third-party content in AI workflows you currently operate or are building — including data used in any FLYING or FreightWaves editorial AI features. Document that audit. The ROSS court's reasoning makes provenance documentation a litigation asset. Two: if you have not already begun conversations with AI companies about licensing your content for training, the fourth-factor analysis in ROSS gives you legal leverage. The court has now said the licensing market is real and legally cognizable. Three: monitor the MDL docket in New York. Reply briefs were due November twentieth. When the court schedules oral argument, that is a material event for the entire publishing industry's AI strategy.
