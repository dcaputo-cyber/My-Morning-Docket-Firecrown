The MDL combines copyright lawsuits filed against OpenAI and Microsoft by The New York Times and other news publishers, Ziff Davis, and a group of authors led by the Authors Guild, seeking to proceed as a class.

[DEEP DIVE]

The Amazon ad auction case is this week's deep dive, and it deserves more than headline treatment.

Here is the structure of the alleged scheme, based on the unredacted complaint. 

Ads are sold on Amazon's platform through so-called second-price auctions, in which businesses set a maximum price they are willing to pay for an ad.

 In a legitimate second-price auction, the winning bidder pays one cent above what the second-highest bidder offered. That is the system advertisers were told they were participating in. 

According to the suit, Amazon inserted "an invented auction participant representing how much Amazon thinks that particular ad slot is worth" — effectively a phantom bid that could override the auction outcome and require the actual winning advertiser to pay more.





According to the complaint, Amazon allegedly increased advertising prices by as much as fifty percent above auction results on ordinary shopping days and by as much as two hundred and fifty percent during major shopping events, including Prime Day and Black Friday.

 When advertisers noticed and asked questions, the response was deceptive. 

When advertisers facing prices inflated by Amazon's hidden surcharges asked if something had changed with respect to the second price auction process, Amazon falsely reassured them that the price increases were the result of increased competition.





Amazon's advertising arm brought in sixty-eight point six billion dollars in 2025, up twenty-two percent, trailing only Google and Meta in digital ads — and ad sales grew another twenty-six percent in the second quarter of 2026, to nineteen point eight billion dollars.

 At those scales, a surcharge mechanism generating incremental revenue shows up as large numbers in internal targets. 

Per the complaint, the head of Amazon Ads told a senior vice president that "I'd like to pull out all of the stops (without doing unnatural acts) to close our gaps to plan."





Amazon has rejected the FTC's broader allegations, arguing that regulators misunderstand how its advertising auctions operate.

 

Amazon says advertisers saved more than eight billion dollars from 2021 to 2025, and that average winning bids fell fifty percent.



Why does this matter to Firecrown as a media company and not just as an observer? Several reasons.

First, the brands in this portfolio run programmatic advertising across multiple platforms, and some of that inventory flows through Amazon's demand-side platform. If the auction mechanics have been misrepresented, the question of what you actually received for your ad spend — and whether the CPMs you thought you were competing at reflect the actual market — is legitimately open.

Second, FreightWaves and its related brands sell media to logistics and transportation advertisers who are themselves heavy Amazon advertisers. If those buyers believe they were overcharged on Amazon, their budgets for the second half of 2026 and into 2027 may tighten or redirect toward media buys where the pricing mechanics are transparent. That is a revenue planning signal.

Third, the legal theory here — that misrepresenting auction mechanics to conceal a surcharge constitutes an unfair or deceptive act under Section 5 of the FTC Act — has implications for any company that sells advertising. The FTC's enforcement position is that the description of the pricing mechanism is itself a material representation. If your own ad products describe a pricing structure, that description needs to match what the system actually does.

Three actions for your agenda. First, pull your agency agreements covering Amazon media buys for the last three years and check whether they include audit rights. The complaint details suggest auditable disparities between auction prices and amounts billed. Second, if Spacestore or any of the Firecrown brands have active Amazon Ads accounts, ask your agency for a reconciliation of cost-per-click against the auction floor the accounts were told applied. Third, review your own ad product documentation — if any Firecrown digital property describes its inventory pricing to buyers, make sure the technical reality of your ad server matches what your sales team and media kit say.
