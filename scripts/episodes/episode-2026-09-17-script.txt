Second item. The FTC rescinded a policy this week and not many people noticed. 

On September ninth, the Federal Trade Commission rescinded its twenty twenty-one policy statement on breaches by health apps and other connected devices. That policy statement had purported to apply the FTC's Health Breach Notification Rule to health apps and connected devices that collect consumer health information.

 

The FTC said it had determined that the statement provided minimal benefit and had been superseded by rulemaking, and the commission said the withdrawal also aligns with White House guidance to pursue a deregulatory agenda and avoid unnecessary use of subregulatory guidance.

 The practical read: the underlying Health Breach Notification Rule, updated in twenty twenty-four, still applies. The policy statement was the interpretive wrapper around that rule. If your digital media properties have wellness, health tracking, or fitness data in their stack — think audience data integrations — the Rule still covers you. The guidance telling you how enforcement would approach it is gone. That is not a green light. It is ambiguity.

 And the agency made clear in March that remediation before being contacted is no longer a shield. 

The March twenty twenty-six PlayOn Sports settlement established that prior remediation is no longer a penalty shield.

Fourth item. The UK picture. 

It has been a year since the Data Use and Access Act gained Royal Assent, and June nineteenth, twenty twenty-six marked the Act's full commencement. It is a significant milestone for UK organizations and the public.

 The ICO now has materially expanded powers. 

Among the new powers is the ability to compel a witness to attend an interview and to request reports of approved persons. The ICO's new draft enforcement procedural guidance sets out the process it will follow when using these powers.

 And the financial stakes went up. 

The DUAA gives the ICO the power to issue fines of up to seventeen point five million pounds or four percent of global turnover under the Privacy and Electronic Communications Regulations.

 For Firecrown brands with any UK audience touchpoint — and the portfolio has them — PECR is now a GDPR-scale financial exposure. That is a material change from the old five hundred thousand pound cap.

Fifth item. The SEC's Regulation S-K overhaul. 

On January thirteenth, twenty twenty-six, the SEC announced plans to conduct a comprehensive review of Regulation S-K, the central framework governing non-financial disclosure requirements for public companies. SEC Chairman Paul Atkins emphasized that the current breadth of S-K has resulted in disclosures that often include significant amounts of immaterial information, which may obscure material facts relevant to investors.

 The review is moving. 

The SEC's next step will likely be a formal rulemaking proposal in twenty twenty-six, with new rules not likely to be effective until the twenty twenty-seven proxy season.

 For a private holding company like Firecrown, this matters one degree removed: it affects the disclosure practices of your publicly traded peers and vendors, and it signals where governance benchmarks are heading. The SEC's direction under Atkins is toward materiality-first, leaner disclosure. 

Companies should continue to focus on AI-related risks in their periodic reports, including regulatory, operational, competitive, cybersecurity, ethical, and third-party risks, and should assess the material impact of AI on business outlook and performance and update risk factors accordingly.

[DEEP DIVE]

Today's deep dive: In re OpenAI Copyright Infringement Litigation and what it means for media companies that both create content and operate digital platforms.

Let us start with the landscape. 

All parties in the consolidated AI copyright infringement cases against OpenAI and Microsoft in New York's Southern District filed motions for summary judgment.

 

The entire docket, now known as In Re Open AI Inc. Copyright Infringement Litigation, case number one twenty-five MD zero three one four three, is being heard in the Southern District of New York.

 There are two tracks: books and news. 

The books track is led by the Authors Guild and named plaintiffs including David Baldacci, John Grisham, George R.R. Martin, and Jodi Picoult, first brought in late twenty twenty-three.

 The news track is led by the New York Times and includes, through consolidation, publications far smaller than the Times.

What are the legal arguments? On the defense side, 

Microsoft and OpenAI told the federal judge that their use of copyrighted materials to develop large language models constitutes fair use, and that AI model outputs transform the ingested information enough to shield them from liability for infringement.

 OpenAI made a second argument specific to the Browse function: that copies taken before publishers updated their robots-dot-txt files were impliedly licensed. 

Copies taken before the block, the defendants argue, were impliedly licensed under Field v. Google.



On the plaintiffs' side, the argument is market harm. 

The plaintiffs argue that the copying of books was nontransformative. Not only was the secretive copying done for the purpose of developing a competing commercial product, they allege, it also meant authors and publishers had no opportunity to earn revenue from AI training licenses.

 The economic argument is sharp: 

the Times spends close to two billion dollars a year producing about half a million works.

 The implied substitution risk is not about verbatim reproduction. It is about functional displacement.

There is a real evidentiary divide developing in the courts. 

In twenty twenty-five, we saw the earliest rulings on the fair-use arguments about AI training in cases involving Meta and Anthropic. In twenty twenty-six, courts are being asked to decide AI training cases involving OpenAI and Google. A judicial consensus is developing that training a general-purpose AI model is highly transformative, a factor favoring the finding of fair use.

 But training is only part of the fight. 

Other issues are the subject of sharp disagreements between courts, and twenty twenty-six is unlikely to bring final answers to copyright questions on AI training. New cases, like Disney's case against Midjourney, are beginning to turn the emphasis from the initial acquisition of training data to claims about the propensity of models to create outputs that are claimed to be infringing.



And licensing is accelerating as the pragmatic backstop. 

Licensing is accelerating and emerging as a potential solution. The Disney-OpenAI agreement shows a trend toward formal licensing of copyrighted material for generative AI use, setting the stage for how entertainment companies monetize IP in AI-driven platforms. Such arrangements may become the industry standard for using copyrighted content in AI training in order to avoid costly and uncertain litigation.



Now let us make this concrete for Firecrown. Your portfolio includes FreightWaves, Trains dot com, FLYING, Boating and Sailing, and Spacestore. Those brands produce original journalism, analysis, and specialist editorial content. You are, in the language of this litigation, a publisher plaintiff class member — even if Firecrown is not a named party.

Three specific exposures. First, the crawl question. 

Common Crawl supplies paywalled content to AI companies despite publisher objections, and news publishers have already targeted Common Crawl, describing it as an AI training data back door.

 If your brand content sits behind a paywall without a properly updated and enforced robots-dot-txt, or without a block on Common Crawl specifically, you may already be in the training data. Audit that now.

Second, the output substitution risk is particularly acute for data-driven journalism. FreightWaves and SONAR produce proprietary freight market intelligence. 

The emerging standard is a market-competition standard: when AI outputs compete with the market for training data, courts are finding infringement.

 The freight intelligence product is precisely the kind of output-competitive content that the court's fourth fair-use factor would scrutinize most carefully.

Third, the licensing opportunity is real and time-sensitive. 

Forbes has launched a dynamic AI paywall as it ramps up post-search commercial diversification, and USA Today's parent is reformatting content to attract more AI licensing deals.

 The window for favorable licensing terms is open while summary judgment is pending. Once the court rules — either way — the leverage shifts.

Three concrete actions for your desk. One: run a content audit across all Firecrown brands. Confirm robots-dot-txt is current, Common Crawl is blocked, and your paywall logs document what has and has not been accessible. Document this now because the timeline of access matters to both the implied license defense and your own potential claims. Two: initiate a licensing posture review. Determine which content assets — SONAR data, FreightWaves analysis, specialist magazine archives — represent the highest licensing value and build a term sheet template before you need it. The market for AI training licenses is active. Three: update your IP risk section in any board governance materials. The SDNY court's summary judgment briefing schedule runs through early November. A ruling could come in Q1 twenty twenty-seven. That is a board-reportable event.

[GC BENCH]

The GC Bench. Legal technology and AI tools for in-house teams.

The adoption number you need: 

in-house generative AI usage is now at eighty-seven percent, nearly double the forty-four percent from a year prior, according to the twenty twenty-six General Counsel Report from FTI Consulting and Relativity.

 But adoption and utility are different things. 

Generative AI use in corporate law departments more than doubled in a year, from twenty-three to fifty-two percent, per the ACC survey. Yet the Bloomberg Law State of Practice twenty twenty-six survey found only twenty-three percent of in-house lawyers use AI tools daily. Plenty of teams have a seat. Far fewer have a tool they depend on.



The product category is bifurcating. 

General-purpose AI providers are entering legal specifically. Anthropic launched Claude for Legal and OpenAI announced Codex for Legal, both positioning as general-purpose AI with a legal mode.

 Against them, purpose-built in-house platforms are competing on citation discipline and workflow depth. 

In-house legal teams have adopted AI faster than outside law firms, and the gap is now large enough to influence how legal services are purchased and delivered.



The practical filter for a GC at a media holding company. 

Sending work to outside counsel solves the capacity problem but creates a spend problem and moves institutional knowledge outside the organization. Neither option scales with the pace the business now moves at, which is why in-house leaders are turning to AI.

 For a generalist GC covering privacy, IP, ad tech, employment, and M-and-A governance across six-plus brands, the highest-value use cases are contract review and regulatory monitoring — not drafting. The drafting tools are maturing. The regulatory scan tools, particularly those that can flag CCPA and DUAA compliance gaps against actual website behavior, are where the operational leverage sits right now.

One caution on the enterprise controls question. 

Generic AI tools like Claude and ChatGPT are flexible and affordable, and attorneys use them unsanctioned. Enterprise controls have to be in place before any client data touches them.

 If Firecrown does not yet have a formal AI use policy for the legal function, that is the first document to draft — before any tool procurement.

 For FreightWaves and SONAR, a fragile market is a high-value market for intelligence. Subscribers need data most when conditions are least predictable. That is a commercial signal worth tracking internally.

Aviation. The numbers are not comfortable for carriers. 

Jet fuel has become the single most powerful variable in airline economics again: the global average price hit one hundred seventy-one dollars per barrel in the first week of September, up nine percent in one week and roughly fifty-seven percent above year-ago levels, forcing carriers like Ryanair to trim growth plans purely to shrink unhedged winter fuel exposure.

 

Demand is holding up far better than feared. July twenty twenty-six global passenger traffic grew zero point two percent year on year despite war-driven capacity cuts in the Middle East, while air cargo posted a stronger three point nine percent increase, with freighters gaining share from belly capacity.

 For FLYING magazine's editorial and advertising model, this is a nuanced picture. General aviation is insulated from airline economics, but fuel cost sensitivity is a shared reader concern. That is content, not just context.

Ocean freight. 

Drewry's World Container Index remained stable at four thousand four hundred seventy-six dollars per forty-foot container as of September tenth.

 

Shanghai to Los Angeles rates climbed to seven thousand one hundred eighty-five dollars per FEU, while carriers have pulled approximately twenty percent of capacity through blank sailings to support rate levels.

 

The first week of September marked the final countdown to a full-scale trade war between the US and Canada. Canadian importers prepared for matching fifteen to fifty percent counter-tariffs on twenty-seven point six billion dollars of American goods, and new US policies now cover fifty-four percent of all American goods imports.

 For Spacestore's e-commerce supply chain, this is a cost-of-goods signal. If Spacestore sources any product from Asia for US-side fulfillment, the transpacific rate environment and blank sailing discipline are direct landed-cost variables right now.
