The second headline is the UK ICO's structural transformation. 

On September 30, 2026, the Information Commissioner's Office became the Information Commission's Office, a subtle but important change introduced by the Data Use and Access Act 2025. From a regulatory perspective the shift from a single decision-maker to a seven-person board marks a significant structural change for the UK data protection regulator.

 

There is no gap or reset in any ongoing investigation, and as the Information Commission's Office the organisation will still be known as the ICO.

 The substantive reform matters more than the nameplate. 

The Data Use and Access Act introduces a new recognised legitimate interests lawful basis, updated cookie and PECR rules, automated decision-making reform, expanded ICO enforcement powers, and fines now aligned with UK GDPR levels of up to four percent of global annual turnover.

 For Firecrown's UK-facing operations, the automated decision-making change is the most consequential. 

The DUAA replaced Article 22 UK GDPR's near-prohibition on solely automated decisions with legal or significantly similar effects with a permissive model allowing organisations to carry out automated decision-making using any lawful basis, provided they implement mandatory safeguards.

 That opens some doors for AI-driven personalization, but it requires documented safeguards. Make sure your UK counsel has mapped those obligations.

[DEEP DIVE]

The AI training copyright litigation landscape, what the Third Circuit ruling actually says, and what a media general counsel should do about it now.

On September 29, the Third Circuit became the first federal appellate court to rule on whether using copyrighted material to train an AI system qualifies as fair use. 

In a long-running and closely watched legal battle between Thomson Reuters and the now-defunct startup ROSS Intelligence, the Third Circuit affirmed a district court's decision that Ross infringed the copyrights in Thomson Reuters's Westlaw headnotes by using them to train an AI-driven legal research tool.

 

The court found that two thousand two hundred forty-three of those headnotes were copied, that each reflected enough editorial judgment to be protected, and that ROSS's use failed three of the four fair use factors.



Let's unpack what the court actually said and what it did not say, because both matter for Firecrown.

What the court said: the editorial selection and curation behind a content product is protectable even when the underlying facts are not. The headnotes at issue were brief summaries, but the attorney editors at Thomson Reuters exercised creative judgment in writing them. 

The court wrote that Thomson Reuters's materials possess a creative spark, and that ROSS aspired to be a direct competitor by using them for a highly similar purpose, and therefore held that the materials are copyrightable and that ROSS's use was not fair.

 The market harm analysis is the part that travels most broadly. 

Market harm under factor four now has appellate backing for the proposition that a licensing market for training data counts as a market, even where none existed before the defendant's conduct. That reasoning does not care whether the model is generative.



What the court did not say: 

The reach of the opinion is limited because the AI technology at the heart of the case was itself relatively limited. ROSS Intelligence's AI product used training data to identify and return existing judicial passages rather than generate new expression.

 Generative AI — the ChatGPT-class systems at issue in the USA Today litigation and a dozen others — raises additional questions about transformative use that this ruling does not resolve. 

That makes it the first federal appellate decision to address fair use in the context of AI training, although the unique facts of the case, including that the copying was done before the advent of generative AI, may minimize the implications.



How does this map to Firecrown? The question is not whether your content is being trained on. Almost certainly some of it is. The question is what position you occupy in the ecosystem and what posture you want to take.

The FreightWaves editorial archive, the FLYING database going back decades, the Trains.com content library, the Boating and Sailing editorial catalog — each of these represents a curated body of specialist knowledge that exhibits exactly the editorial judgment the Third Circuit found protectable. Freight market analysis, aviation incident reporting, marine technical commentary — these are not commodity text. They are the product of journalists and editors exercising selection and verification judgment over years. That is the creative spark the court identified.

At the same time, Firecrown is not a scale news organization with a litigation budget that matches the New York Times. The USA Today lawsuit, seeking two hundred fifty million dollars, reflects a company choosing to fight. Other publishers are choosing to license. 

Companies like Conde Nast are licensing content to OpenAI and Amazon while building subscriptions and direct audience relationships as primary growth levers.



The practical actions for a Firecrown general counsel right now are three.

First, conduct a content rights audit across all five brands. Map what content Firecrown owns outright, what it licenses from contributors, and what sits in any ambiguous zone with freelance agreements that predate AI. The strength of a copyright claim depends entirely on clean chain of title. If your contributor agreements from 2018 or 2019 did not expressly address AI training rights, that is a gap.

Second, update your robots.txt files and terms of service on every Firecrown property to include an explicit prohibition on scraping for AI training purposes. This does not stop all scraping, but it establishes that your opt-out preference was clear — which matters both for the licensing market the Third Circuit recognized and for any future litigation posture.

Third, consider whether Firecrown's niche-audience brands are candidates for a licensing conversation with one or more AI platform operators. The FreightWaves content, in particular, is the kind of domain-specific, real-world, verified data that AI companies need to reduce hallucinations in professional applications. That has actual economic value. The question is whether you negotiate for it or wait for someone to take it without asking.

A brief on what the businesses you serve are actually seeing in the market this week.

On freight: the trucking market entering the fourth quarter is producing the kind of numbers FreightWaves has been anticipating for most of the year. 

Truckload freight rates are expected to reach their highest levels in more than four years during the fourth quarter, according to the latest TD Cowen AFS Freight Index. The report projects truckload rates will climb to twenty-three-point-four percent above their January 2018 baseline in the fourth quarter, an increase of fourteen-point-seven percent year over year.

 The important distinction is what is driving this. 

The increase is being driven largely by shrinking trucking capacity, elevated diesel prices, and regulatory enforcement actions rather than a meaningful recovery in freight demand.



SONAR data confirms the structural story. 

As of October 2026, new trucking capacity is not entering the freight market, even after a full year of elevated tender rejections. SONAR data shows tractors in new for-hire fleets fell to nineteen thousand five hundred twenty in August, while the SONAR Truckload Rejection Index held near fourteen percent, about three times its 2023 to 2025 range.

 

Rejections are up two hundred sixty-eight percent in three years, while volume is up only nine percent. The market is tight because trucks are leaving, not because freight is surging.

 

A carrier looking to add a truck in 2026 faces insurance costs that have risen faster than freight rates, meaning the natural rebalancing mechanism — where higher rates encourage more capacity — is broken.

 For FreightWaves and SONAR, this market condition is validation of the data platform's thesis. Shippers and carriers need real-time intelligence precisely when markets are structurally dislocated.
