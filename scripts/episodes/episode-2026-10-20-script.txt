[DEEP DIVE]

Now the deep dive. Ten minutes on what the Third Circuit ruling in Thomson Reuters v. ROSS Intelligence actually means, what it does not mean, and what three actions a GC at a media company should take before the end of the fourth quarter.

Start with the facts. 

Thomson Reuters sued the now-defunct AI startup ROSS Intelligence, claiming ROSS took headnotes — short descriptions of important legal issues appearing before judicial opinions — from its Westlaw database, using a third party called LegalEase Solutions to gain access after Westlaw refused direct access. ROSS then used that data to train an AI product designed to compete directly with Westlaw.





On September 29th, 2026, a three-judge Third Circuit panel affirmed a district court decision, making this the first federal appellate ruling on fair use in the context of AI training data.

 

In ruling for Thomson Reuters, the court held that the Westlaw headnotes are sufficiently original and thus protected by copyright, and that use of those headnotes to train a competing product did not qualify as fair use.



Walk through the four-factor analysis the court applied. 

The court concluded that the use was highly commercial and only minimally transformative, and it found harm to both Westlaw's existing legal-research market and a developing market for licensing headnotes as AI training data.

 

On the third factor — amount and substantiality — the court found that ROSS copied entire headnotes without sufficiently transformative justification. The court rejected ROSS's argument that the copied material represented only a small fraction of Thomson Reuters' collection, explaining that each headnote was independently copyrightable.

 Three of four factors went against ROSS. 

With three of the four factors weighing against fair use, the Third Circuit affirmed partial summary judgment for Thomson Reuters.



Now, the limits — and these limits matter enormously for generative AI companies. 

The court emphasized that its holding was limited to the non-generative AI system at issue, leaving broader questions concerning generative AI training unresolved.

 

The opinion does not establish that every use of copyrighted material in AI training is infringing or that every AI-training fair-use defense must fail. It confirms more specifically that the involvement of machine learning does not displace ordinary copyright analysis where protectable expression is copied and used for a closely similar competitive purpose.



So what carries forward from this ruling? The most legally durable holding is on market harm. 

Market harm under factor four now has appellate backing for the proposition that a licensing market for training data counts as a market, even where none existed before the defendant's conduct. That reasoning does not care whether the model is generative.

 That is significant. It means that any AI company that trains on a publisher's content is now on notice that a court can recognize a nascent licensing market as a real market for fair use purposes — regardless of whether that licensing market existed at the time of the copying.

Layer on the USA TODAY filing from October 8th. 

The complaint alleges that OpenAI accessed the publishers' copyrighted material to train consumer-facing products such as ChatGPT. The plaintiffs also allege that OpenAI's internal communications show that the company deliberately targeted news content and knew its models could reproduce that material.

 If those internal communications show what the plaintiffs claim, the willfulness analysis in the USA TODAY case will look very different from the ROSS situation, where ROSS was a non-generative system run by a startup that is now defunct. The USA TODAY case involves a well-capitalized generative AI company with extensive contemporaneous records.

The Anthropic settlement from earlier in 2026 adds another data point. 

Even where a court drew a hard line around how training data was obtained and stored — specifically Anthropic's practice of retaining pirated digital copies of books — the case ultimately settled for one-point-five billion dollars, with individual payouts estimated near three thousand dollars per infringed work.

 The settlement arithmetic is clarifying. If you are a publisher with a large archive, you have a rough valuation framework for what the market thinks individual works are worth in this litigation context.

Now, the three concrete actions a media and publishing GC should take before year-end.

Action one: conduct a content asset audit with AI-licensing monetization in mind. The Thomson Reuters ruling confirms that a licensing market for training data is cognizable. The Google News AI pilot confirms that licensing revenue — however uneven at this stage — is real. 

An IAB survey of one hundred ten US publishers found fifty-one percent had signed AI licensing deals, but only twenty percent of small independents had done so.

 If your editorial archives have not been surveyed for their AI-training value, that gap is now a governance deficiency. Identify the highest-specificity, lowest-substitutability content across the Firecrown portfolio — the proprietary freight data analysis at FreightWaves, the technical pilot content at FLYING, the marine market intelligence in the Boating and Sailing titles. That content is the most defensible and the most potentially licensable.

Action two: audit your inbound AI vendor contracts. Every SaaS tool your teams use — editorial workflow software, ad tech platforms, analytics vendors — likely contains provisions about how user-generated or user-uploaded content is used for model training. Post-ROSS, any clause that permits a vendor to use your proprietary content to train a product that competes with your data products carries legal risk that courts are now willing to quantify. That clause review belongs on the Q4 legal calendar.

Action three: build a documented opt-in stance for web crawlers and review your robots.txt implementation. 

For businesses developing AI systems, the decision underscores the importance of examining what training materials contain, how they were obtained, the purpose for which they are used and the relationship between the resulting product and the copyright owner's market.

 The inverse of that analysis applies to a publisher protecting its own content: you want a clear, documented record of what crawlers you have permitted, what you have blocked, and when those decisions were made. If litigation arises, that record is your contemporaneous evidence of the licensing market you intended to create.

[INDUSTRY ANGLE]

The industry angle. Freight, aviation, and marine — the sectors that sit at the core of Firecrown's audience and the economic environment surrounding their readers.

On freight. The SONAR data through mid-October is striking. 

The SONAR Truckload Rejection Index hit fourteen percent in October — a significant development outside normal seasonal patterns. This is not driven by a demand spike, but rather by persistent market imbalance and insufficient supply.

 

As of the week of October 12th, national spot truckload rates are approximately three dollars and nine cents per mile for dry van and three dollars and sixty-five cents per mile for both reefer and flatbed, all-in including fuel.

 

Craig Fuller, in a SONAR analysis published October 13th, noted that contract rates are climbing past recession highs and spot rates are trending toward peak. Diesel prices topped six dollars and fifty cents per gallon — an all-time record — before pulling back modestly.

 

Fuller cited freight market analysis suggesting that the market strength is not primarily demand-driven — it is related to capacity and relative supply dynamics.



The structural capacity question is Dalilah's Law. 

The Dalilah Law, the Senate bill introduced by Senator Jim Banks of Indiana following President Trump's call during the State of the Union, would trigger a sharp, immediate contraction in trucking capacity if enacted, potentially igniting a trucking super cycle with overnight rate surges amid severely tight supply.

 

The bill has cleared the House Transportation and Infrastructure Committee and is soon expected to be put to a vote before the full House.

 For FreightWaves readers and SONAR subscribers, the regulatory trajectory here is the rate story for 2027.
