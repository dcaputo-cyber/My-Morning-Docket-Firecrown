[DEEP DIVE]

Now the deep dive. The Department of Justice statement of interest in the OpenAI copyright litigation, and what it means for a media company that both publishes original content and evaluates AI tools built on that content.

On September first, 

the Department of Justice filed a Statement of Interest in the Southern District of New York asking the court to hold that training large language models on copyrighted written works qualifies as fair use.

 

The filing lands in the consolidated copyright litigation against OpenAI and Microsoft, and it goes further than the parties themselves have, arguing that a ruling against the AI developers would distort copyright doctrine, slow domestic innovation, and weaken United States competitiveness and national security.



Let's be precise about what this filing does and does not do. 

The DOJ filing materially changes the arguments before the court by urging a fair-use view of training-stage copying and tying that position to innovation and national security. It does not decide the case, establish that every dataset was lawfully acquired or stored, or resolve questions about model outputs and market substitution.

 

The filing advocates a fair-use position for training-stage copying while leaving acquisition, storage, and output questions distinct, with the court's ruling still pending.



Why does this framing matter for Firecrown? Two reasons. First, as a publisher, you are a potential plaintiff class member in consolidated litigation that now has the federal government intervening against your legal position. Second, as a company evaluating or deploying AI tools built on foundation models, the DOJ's framing actually benefits you operationally, if and when the court adopts it.

The precedential backdrop is important. 

In Thomson Reuters v. Ross, the court rejected a fair use defense for the use of copyrighted works to train an AI legal search tool, holding that Ross was using copyrighted headnotes to create a competing legal research product, which was not transformative, and that Ross's tool served as a market substitute, with the court noting the effect on a potential market for AI training data.

 That ruling was a win for rights holders. The DOJ is now asking a different court to go the other direction on substantially similar facts, but in a different industry context.



In January of this year, Judge Sidney Stein in the Southern District of New York affirmed an order requiring OpenAI to produce twenty million de-identified ChatGPT conversation logs, with plaintiffs having originally sought one hundred twenty million.

 Discovery is still running. The case is not close to resolution.

For the Bartz v. Anthropic settlement, 

notable developments came in August and September twenty twenty-five when the case was certified as a class action and preliminary approval of a one point five billion dollar settlement was announced.

 

Final approval came July twentieth, twenty twenty-six, with a first per-work payment due on or before November of this year.

 That payment is now the market reference point for how AI developers price content liability exposure. One point five billion dollars across a class of nearly half a million eligible works produces an average payment that is not going to replace a licensing stream for any serious publisher.

What should a general counsel at a media holding company take from this? Three concrete actions.

First, audit your content licensing posture. If FreightWaves, FLYING, Trains.com, or any other Firecrown brand has content that has been scraped and used in AI training, you are a potential class member in the consolidated OpenAI MDL or in future actions. Know which works are registered with the Copyright Office. Registered works carry enhanced statutory damages. Unregistered works do not. The registration window matters.

Second, for any AI vendor contracts you are reviewing or renewing, add explicit representations about training data provenance. The DOJ framing protects large foundation model developers at the training stage. It does not extend to downstream vendors who fine-tune on your content without authorization. Distinguish those layers in your vendor agreements.

Third, pay attention to the fourth factor in the fair use analysis: market substitution. The DOJ argument on training-stage copying is stronger when the AI output does not substitute for the original. If an AI product built on Firecrown content produces freight market analysis that competes with SONAR or FreightWaves editorial, that substitution argument collapses in your favor. Document any instances of substitutive output now, before you need them in litigation.

The DOJ's entry into this case is significant. It is not dispositive. The court still decides.
