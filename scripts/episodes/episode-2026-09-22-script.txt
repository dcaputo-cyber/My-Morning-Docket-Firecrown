[DEEP DIVE]

We are going to spend the next ten minutes on the DOJ's September first statement of interest in the OpenAI consolidated copyright litigation, and what it means for a company sitting in Firecrown's exact position: a media holding company that produces original content and also, as a publisher, has an interest in how that content is used by AI systems.

Here is the factual foundation. 

On September first, the Department of Justice filed a Statement of Interest in the Southern District of New York, asking the court to hold that training large language models on copyrighted written works qualifies as fair use. The filing lands in the consolidated copyright litigation against OpenAI and Microsoft, and it goes further than the parties themselves have, arguing that a ruling against the AI developers would distort copyright doctrine, slow domestic innovation, and weaken United States competitiveness and national security.





The litigation encompasses not only The New York Times' December 2023 suit against OpenAI and Microsoft, but every publisher, outlet, and author currently in litigation under the MDL's umbrella.



Before we assess the implications, a critical point of precision. 

For counsel, avoid the shortcut that the DOJ says training is legal. The narrower and supportable statement is that the DOJ advocates fair use for the training-stage copying question in this litigation.

 That is not a judicial holding. It is the executive branch's stated position in active litigation, and it is subject to change if the administration changes. Judge Stein still has to rule.

What the filing does do is shift the negotiating terrain. 

For creators, the strategic picture after September first has clarified, even if it has not improved. The court-based avenue for compensation remains open, but it now runs against the executive branch's declared position and through a judiciary being shaped by a president who has made domestic AI dominance an explicit policy priority. The more tractable routes are legislative and contractual.





Congress has not yet passed legislation addressing AI training and copyright — a gap the Copyright Office's May 2025 report explicitly identified as a policy question for Congress to resolve.



Now, what does this mean specifically for Firecrown? You are managing both sides of the ledger simultaneously.

On the content producer side: FreightWaves publishes proprietary market intelligence. FLYING publishes original reporting and technical analysis. Trains.com and Boating and Sailing produce specialty editorial that is difficult to replicate from public data. Each of those bodies of work has value as training data, and the DOJ's position — that training-stage copying is transformative — does not eliminate the licensing market, it arguably incentivizes it. 

AI companies now face direct incentives to negotiate licensing deals. A settled licensing relationship is cheaper and more predictable than litigation risk, particularly when courts are beginning to examine these questions closely.

 That means Firecrown should not read the DOJ brief as a reason to stand down. It should read it as a reason to get ahead of the licensing conversation.



For a publisher, the practical advice is to preserve separate evidence about source acquisition, permissions, licensing, retention, and observed outputs.

 If any AI developer has been scraping FreightWaves content, Trains.com editorial, or FLYING features without a licensing arrangement, document that now. The litigation will eventually produce discovery, and well-organized evidence of scraping without permission is the basis for negotiating leverage even if the fair use question ultimately goes to the developers.

On the AI-product side: Firecrown, through SONAR and related data products, is in some respects both a publisher and a data company. To the extent that SONAR or any Firecrown entity trains models on data — including licensed third-party data — 

it matters to record how the provider or customer obtained the works, how copies were prepared, what training or retrieval process used them, what the resulting model retains, and how the buyer uses the output.

 That documentation framework is your compliance posture regardless of how the litigation resolves.

The third dimension is your vendor relationships. If you use any AI tools internally — for editorial assistance, contract review, market analysis — and those tools were trained on data whose provenance is uncertain, the DOJ filing does not insulate you from downstream liability. The filing addresses training-stage copying by the developer; it says nothing about contractual representations, licensing warranties, or indemnification from your SaaS vendors.

Three concrete actions for this week. First: have your IP team audit each Firecrown brand's published archive to understand what content exists, whether any license or terms of service prohibit AI training, and whether your existing agreements with hosting platforms or syndication partners inadvertently grant training rights. Second: ask any AI vendor you currently use to provide written confirmation of how their training datasets were assembled and what indemnification, if any, they offer for copyright claims arising from output. If they cannot answer the first question, that is itself a risk signal. Third: brief Craig Fuller and the executive team on the DOJ's position in plain language. The instinct may be to treat this as good news for AI generally. The nuanced read — and the legally defensible posture — is that it accelerates the timeline for licensing rather than eliminating the need for it.
