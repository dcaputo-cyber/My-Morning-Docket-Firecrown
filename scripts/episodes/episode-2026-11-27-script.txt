[DEEP DIVE]

The ruling that deserves a full ten minutes this morning is the Third Circuit's decision in Thomson Reuters versus ROSS Intelligence, handed down at the end of last week. I want to give you the architecture of the ruling and then work through the implications for a media company that publishes proprietary data and editorial content.

Here is what happened at the case level. ROSS Intelligence built a competing legal research platform by using Westlaw headnotes — editorially curated summaries of judicial holdings — to train its AI model. Thomson Reuters sued for copyright infringement. ROSS argued fair use. 

The Third Circuit's decision found that ROSS's use of those Westlaw headnotes to build a competing legal research product was not protected fair use, because it harmed both the existing market for legal research and the potential market for licensing training data.

 

The court left unresolved whether AI-generated works that compete with but do not directly copy original content can constitute cognizable harm under copyright law.



The core of the analysis is the fourth factor of the fair use test — market effect. The court's reasoning was that ROSS was not just learning from Westlaw; it was building a product designed to replace Westlaw. 

The authoring judge, Tamika Montgomery-Reeves, wrote that under ROSS's framing the case might appear to concern the future of AI legal technology, but that appearances can be deceiving, and that in truth this is no more than an ordinary copyright case.

 That framing matters. The court is saying it did not need to resolve the AI-training question as a novel category. It applied conventional fair use factors and found the fourth factor dispositive.

Now put this next to the broader litigation landscape. 

OpenAI published excerpts from twenty amicus filings urging a New York court to find that training large language models on copyrighted works is fair use.

 

In the consolidated OpenAI copyright litigation in New York, Judge Sidney Stein allowed fifty-six amicus briefs on the cross-motions for summary judgment, and a November twelfth order gave the plaintiffs until November twenty-fifth to seek summary judgment on output claims.

 

The Trump administration has taken the position in that litigation that AI training may qualify as fair use.

 And just this week, 

a Canadian federal court struck a proposed class action claim by Canadian authors against Meta, with Justice Gagné ruling that activities occurring entirely outside Canada are beyond the scope of that country's Copyright Act.

 

Separately, the New York Times and the Daily News have each filed copyright claims against Brave Software in the Southern District of New York, extending news publishers' litigation to AI-powered search.



What does this mean for Firecrown specifically? Three things.

First, the ROSS ruling establishes a market harm framework that is favorable to data publishers. FreightWaves and SONAR generate proprietary, high-frequency freight market data. If an AI model were trained on that data and the resulting tool competed directly with SONAR as a market intelligence product, the ROSS analysis is your friend. The key evidentiary work is documenting: one, that the data is editorially curated or analytically transformed — not raw commodity data; two, that a licensing market for that data exists or is foreseeable; and three, that the infringing use would substitute for, not merely reference, the original product. That documentation should be in your data agreements and your SONAR terms of service today, not in a declaration after a lawsuit is filed.

Second, the ROSS ruling does not resolve the broader training question. The court was explicit that it was ruling on a commercial competitor use case. The SDNY litigation — which involves news publishers and may ultimately reach the Supreme Court — is on a different trajectory, and the Justice Department's position supporting OpenAI adds political complexity. Do not read ROSS as settling the matter.

Third, your existing content licensing structure matters enormously. If your content agreements with wire services, freelancers, or syndication partners do not address AI training rights, you are likely both unprotected as a licensor and potentially exposed as a licensee. A short-form audit of your top twenty content agreements — looking specifically for AI-training clauses and sublicensing restrictions — is the most useful thing your team can do before the SDNY summary judgment arguments get scheduled.

Three concrete actions for the week ahead.

One: direct your IP counsel to review the ROSS decision for specific language on the fourth fair use factor's treatment of training data licensing markets, and map that language onto your SONAR and FreightWaves data licensing agreements. Any agreement that does not restrict AI training use is a gap.

The question of platform liability for fraudulent impersonation advertising could directly affect any brand-advertising product Firecrown runs.

Three: for FLYING, Boating and Sailing, and Trains.com, review the affirmative coverage those brands produce about AI-powered aircraft systems, autonomous vessels, and rail automation. If you are quoting company executives about AI capabilities in those stories, you have a reputational and potentially a legal interest in accuracy standards that parallel what the SEC is now enforcing in public company disclosures. Editorial accuracy standards and legal risk management are not separate conversations anymore.
