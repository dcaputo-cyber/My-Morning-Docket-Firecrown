California announced seventeen-point-one million dollars in CCPA fines in 2026, and twelve of the fourteen CCPA settlements since 2022 involved broken, missing, or hard-to-use opt-outs.

 The common thread across the FedEx, Hertz, and Filson actions this month is not that companies were unaware of GPC — it's that their infrastructure failed to honor it in practice. 

CalPrivacy alleged that Federal Express falsely stated that it did not sell or share personal information when it participated in cross-context behavioral advertising.

 That is a knowing misrepresentation combined with a technical failure. The agency is treating that combination more seriously than a pure compliance gap.

Here is why this matters structurally for Firecrown. You operate across freight intelligence, aviation enthusiast media, boating and sailing titles, rail journalism through Trains.com, and an e-commerce property in Spacestore. Each brand runs its own analytics stack and advertising relationships. Each has California visitors. Each is therefore within scope. 

Recent enforcement actions demonstrate regulators' increasing willingness to challenge advertising practices, age-assurance measures, and the processing of sensitive personal information. Privacy leaders should prioritize vendor and ad tech reviews, AI governance frameworks, sensitive data controls, and privacy-by-design initiatives, as these areas appear most likely to attract regulatory attention in the coming months.



The GPC signal failure that brought down FedEx and Hertz is specifically a cross-device and cross-service problem. A user sends a GPC opt-out signal from a browser on one device. The signal is received on the website. But the company's mobile app, its connected TV property, its ad exchange integrations — none of them see it. The user is still being tracked and monetized across those surfaces. That is the violation. 

Disney's two-point-seven-five-million-dollar settlement earlier this year arose from exactly this structure: opt-outs did not apply across services and devices, and there was no in-app opt-out on connected TVs.



For Firecrown, the specific risk surfaces to audit are three. First, any header bidding or programmatic advertising integration that operates on multiple domains or apps. Second, any retargeting or behavioral audience product that syncs data across the FreightWaves web platform and the SONAR subscription product. Third, Spacestore's e-commerce analytics, which almost certainly involves third-party tracking tools that are commercially standard but potentially non-compliant with GPC.

There is also a California amendment worth flagging for your 2027 compliance calendar. 

AB 566, the California Opt Me Out Act, signed in October 2025, is in force January 1, 2027. It adds a requirement that any business that develops or maintains a browser must include an easy-to-find setting that sends an opt-out preference signal.

 That is primarily aimed at browser vendors, but the downstream obligation for publishers is that GPC signals arriving through those compliant browsers will carry heightened legal weight — and the CalPrivacy enforcement division has already signaled it will act on them.
