[DEEP DIVE]

The New York State Bar's AI Billing Rules: What Every GC Needs to Know Before the Next Outside Counsel Invoice.



The New York State Bar Association, on November second, issued a report calling for restrictions on how much lawyers can bill clients for time saved using artificial intelligence tools or for time spent learning how to use them. The Association's Committee on Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technologies classified the cost of buying and maintaining AI software as an overhead expense — passable as a disbursement only when tied to a specific client matter and when the basis for the charge has been disclosed to the client.





The Association's governing body adopted the report on Saturday, November first.

 This is now formal policy guidance for New York-admitted attorneys, which covers most sophisticated outside counsel that a media holding company would retain.

Let's unpack what this means in practice. 

The report advises that attorneys may charge fees for time spent verifying an AI tool's output, but not for learning how to use it. Acquiring an AI tool is an overhead expense, so its cost may not be billed to a client unless it is for a service specially incurred for that specific case.



This creates three distinct billing categories that your outside counsel agreements should now explicitly address. First: tool subscription costs. A law firm's enterprise Harvey or CoCounsel subscription is overhead. It cannot appear as a line item on your invoice. If a firm specially procures a bespoke AI tool for a Firecrown matter — say, a document review platform configured to your data room — that arguably crosses into recoverable disbursement territory, but only with upfront disclosure.

Second: learning time. If a junior associate spent forty hours in September learning to prompt an AI contract analysis tool, those hours cannot be billed. This matters because AI learning curves at law firms are real and ongoing, and without explicit guidance, billing partners might absorb those hours into matter files without attribution.

Third: verification time. This one runs the other way. 

The report notes that lawyers generally don't have to tell clients about AI use for administrative tasks — such as word processing, spelling, grammar checks, calendaring, or timekeeping — provided that use doesn't affect fees.

 But if an attorney spends time reviewing AI-generated output for accuracy before filing or submitting a contract, that review time is billable. The logic is sound: the professional judgment and verification step is legal work. The machine generating the first draft is not.



Context worth noting: in-house AI adoption has crossed into majority territory in ways that are consequential. The twenty twenty-six General Counsel Report from FTI Consulting and Relativity puts in-house generative AI usage at eighty-seven percent — nearly double the forty-four percent from a year prior.

 So we are past the novelty phase. The question is no longer whether to adopt AI; it is how to govern the financial and ethical dimensions of adoption across both internal teams and external counsel relationships.

There is a parallel development in legal tech worth flagging. 

Ross Intelligence has urged the full Third Circuit to revisit a copyright decision concluding it was not protected by fair use when it trained its AI legal research platform on Thomson Reuters' Westlaw headnotes, arguing that "at every turn, the panel asked the wrong question."

 That appeal matters because it could reset the legal framework for training any professional AI tool on commercially licensed legal content — which affects every vendor pitching AI legal research tools to your legal operations team.



Thomson Reuters' own CoCounsel, meanwhile, published a development note today describing its integration of an agentic harness using the Claude SDK — meaning the system can now plan, delegate, and orchestrate across multiple tools and content sources in real time, with users describing what they want to accomplish and the system determining how to get there.

 That is a meaningful step beyond task execution into autonomous legal workflow management.

Three concrete actions for a GC at a media holding company.

First: audit your outside counsel engagement letters immediately. Add an AI billing clause that specifies tool subscription costs are overhead, that AI learning time is non-billable, and that any AI disbursement requires advance written disclosure of the tool name, its cost, and its application to the specific matter. Many firms are ahead of this, but many are not.

Second: on the internal AI governance side, formalize your legal department's AI tool inventory. 

Contract review tools like Checkbox and Ironclad are replacing work that previously required outside review at four hundred to eight hundred dollars an hour.

 Document what your team uses, what it doesn't send outside the firewall, and what confidentiality representations you have from each vendor.

Third: review your New York-admitted outside counsel's AI disclosure policies before the end of the year. The NYSBA guidance is advisory but signals where bar enforcement is heading. Firms that have not updated their engagement terms to address AI are carrying policy risk on your matters.

The freight market through SONAR's lens: Craig Fuller's two economies and what they mean for Firecrown's core audience.



Craig Fuller, founder and CEO of FreightWaves, told Adam Taggart on the Thoughtful Money podcast that freight data, which typically leads government economic figures by about six months, shows no sign that the AI data-center construction boom is slowing.

 

Fuller described himself as "lukewarm" on the overall economy, characterizing it as a tale of two transportation economies. The freight data shows the consumer slowing while the industrial economy is the strongest he has seen in perhaps a decade. Because today's industrial expansion is driven by forces largely disconnected from the consumer, the two are not pulling each other up or down the way they traditionally would.



The market data behind that observation is stark. 

SONAR's current readings show the Truckload Rejection Index at nearly thirteen percent, up two-point-nine percent on the week, and the National Truckload Index at three dollars and eight cents per mile — a forty percent increase from a year ago.





Elevated fuel prices, tight capacity, and peak-season fees are pushing freight rates to historic highs. The fourth-quarter TD Cowen and AFS Freight Index projects the highest truckload pricing in more than four years and record-high rates for LTL and parcel.

 

The LTL rate-per-pound index is projected to reach eighty-two-point-five percent above its January twenty eighteen baseline — the first quarterly reading on record to exceed eighty percent.





Fuller has noted that the market came out of a long freight recession in November twenty twenty-five, and capacity is now shrinking faster than demand is growing.

 

The central warning from SONAR's Zach Strickland is that the current freight cycle is nowhere near its conclusion, and that most cycles run far longer than market participants expect.



For FreightWaves as a business and for SONAR as a subscription product, this environment is favorable. Shippers and logistics managers who need real-time rate intelligence are paying for it, and 

the U.S. Department of Transportation has officially signed on as a customer of SONAR to harness high-frequency freight market data for monitoring market health.

 A federal agency anchoring the SONAR subscriber base is both a revenue signal and a credibility marker for the platform.



In LTL specifically, Roadrunner this week announced a new outbound Canada-to-U.S. service alongside a five-point-nine percent general rate increase taking effect November second.

 That rate increase, landing in the same week as the TD Cowen index projections, confirms that carriers have pricing power and are using it.

The marine dimension: 

container freight rates are forecast to decline twenty-nine-point-six percent on average over the near-term forecast period as idle capacity rises, with the orderbook-to-fleet ratio approaching forty percent — materially outpacing TEU-mile demand growth.

 That is a structurally different story from trucking. For Boating and Sailing's audience, the relevant signal is the decarbonization cost curve: 

from twenty twenty-six, shipping companies must account for one hundred percent of applicable emissions on voyages linked to EU ports under the EU Emissions Trading Scheme, up from seventy percent in twenty twenty-five.

 That regulatory step-up is a live editorial and commercial angle for the marine brands.
